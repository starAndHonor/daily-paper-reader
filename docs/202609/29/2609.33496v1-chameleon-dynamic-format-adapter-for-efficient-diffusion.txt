ABSTRACT 

A very popular way to enable image generation on memory-constrained accelera-tors using modern spatiotemporal generative models is post-training quantization (PTQ ). However, every existing diffusion PTQ scheme shares an unstated com-mitment: the number format is fixed in advance, and only the scale, zero point, or the per-layer bit-width is allowed to move. This is unfortunate, because at a fixed bit-width the best format depends on the distribution being encoded, and that distribution differs from tensor to tensor: across weight channels, across lay-ers, and – in a denoising model – along the diffusion timestep , where activations slide from heavy-tailed and noise-dominated at t → T to tightly clustered and structured at t → 0. One format for the whole network is therefore mispriced for most of its tensors, and re-tuning a scale cannot repair a format mismatch. In this work, we propose a PTQ framework, which we call Chameleon , that holds the bit-width fixed and treats the number format itself as a discrete vari-able, chosen per weight channel and per (layer, timestep bucket) activation tensor. Chameleon draws activation formats from a palette of {INT8 , FP8 E4M3 , FP8 E5M2 , MXFP8 , MXINT8 } and weight formats from {INT8 , MXINT8 } at 8 bits or 

{INT4 , NF4 , FP4 E2M1 , MXINT4 , MXFP4 } at 4 bits, choosing activation for-mats ahead of time from two cheap statistics – empirical activation kurtosis and closed-form diffusion SNR ¯αt/(1 − ¯αt) – stored in a per-(layer, timestep bucket) lookup table, and weight formats offline by reconstruction error. An architectural fork adapts the same selection layer to each model family, so each model exercises the palette as far as its own statistics warrant: weights and per-(layer, bucket) acti-vations on multi-step SDXL , weights alone where a single sampler step leaves no timestep axis. We evaluate Chameleon across three diffusion regimes – multi-step 

SDXL , single-step SDXL-Turbo , and the PixArt -α Diffusion Transformer – on COCO-2014. Chameleon achieves the best FID for all six backbone ×bit-width settings, while preserving semantic alignment with the text prompts ( CLIP within 

0.24 of the FP16 reference in every setting, and the best of all quantized methods at W 4A8). Adapting the format, rather than only its scale, recovers distributional fidelity at 4-bit weights while holding prompt adherence at the FP16 level. 1

1 INTRODUCTION 

The frontier of generative AI lies not in the cloud but on the edge (Zhou et al., 2025). Latent diffusion models, few-step distilled models, and diffusion transformers ( DiT s) all generate images from pure noise by stepping through a learned vector field for tens to hundreds of forward passes through a network holding billions of parameters: SDXL alone has roughly 2.6 billion, and text encoding and the variational auto-encoder add another billion. Replacing 16-bit numerics with 8-, 6-, or 4-bit ones – post-training quantization ( PTQ ) – is the standard response, cutting memory footprint and latency while raising throughput (Nagel et al., 2021; Frantar et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023). This paper proposes a PTQ scheme for the iteratively denoising generators in that list. The key idea is that while weights can be statically quantized, activations should not be, because of the iterative nature of the inference pipeline. We call our dynamic format adapter framework Chameleon: at a fixed bit-width it chooses a number format per tensor – per weight channel, per layer, and per timestep bucket where a timestep axis exists – using cheaply computed statistical heuristics. Prior work has contributed a great deal to this space (Section 2), but the four diffusion PTQ baselines we compare against – Q-Diffusion , PTQ4DM , MixDQ and Q-DiT – all take quantization to mean rounding to the nearest INT8 format. This is suboptimal for at least two reasons. First, at a fixed bit-width the format is still free, and the right choice follows the distribution being encoded: Kuzmin et al. (2022) show that the signal-to-quantization-noise ratio ( SQNR ) at a given width is a function of both that distribution and the format, and Zhang et al. (2024) turn this into a per-layer INT -or-FP choice for LLM s. Tensors within one network differ in shape – across weight channels and across layers – so no single format is right for all of them. Denoising networks add a second axis: the activations of a single layer are themselves a parametric family of distributions indexed by timestep t (Li et al., 2023). At t → T , activation tensors are dominated by Gaussian noise, and the internal activations they induce are heavy-tailed, with kurtosis ( κ) above 5; at t → 0

they are dominated by the structured signal of a near-convergence latent, with κ often below 3. The format that is right at t = 0 is wrong at t = T . Re-tuning the scale of INT8 cannot make it tolerate heavy tails, any more than re-scaling FP8 E5M2 can give its logarithmic bins the density to render fine texture. The fix is to change formats along the schedule. 2Second, microscaling formats – MXFP8, MXINT8, MXINT4 (OCP Microscaling Formats Work-ing Group, 2023; Rouhani et al., 2023) – are now hardware features on Blackwell-class accelerators, with FP8 already native on Hopper and Ada. One shared 8-bit exponent per 32 elements lets a single tensor hold values spanning very different magnitudes without one global scale covering them all. This is exactly what the concatenations at every UNet shortcut need, where shallow skip features meet deep upsampled features. Measured on SDXL , the tensor entering a shortcut conv1 spans a peak-to-median per-channel range of 58 × at the median layer and up to 163 × (Appendix A.11), so one per-tensor scale must cover outliers that most channels never approach. Q-Diffusion split the computational graph for this; with MX formats, one need not. These two observations – that the right format depends on the tensor, and on t where a timestep axis exists, and that the format should sometimes be a microscaling block format – are the substance of this paper. Figure 2 sketches the resulting framework end-to-end. We present Chameleon , a PTQ framework that treats the number format as a discrete variable, chosen per weight channel and per ( layer , timestep bucket ) activation tensor from the palettes of Section 4.2. Two cheap statistics – the empirical kurtosis κl(t) and the closed-form diffusion SNR –decide it offline, and the result costs one indexed read per layer per step. 1.1 MOTIVATION 

PTQ research for time-stepped image generators is architecture-specific, but the three lines of work converge on one observation: the activation distributions inside a denoising network are non-stationary along the diffusion trajectory, and that non-stationarity is the dominant source of quantiza-tion error. What none of those four adjusts is the number format itself. Q-Diffusion , PTQ4DM ,

MixDQ , and Q-DiT all commit up-front to a single format – invariably INT8 – and route their design effort into scales, zero points, per-layer bit-widths, group sizes, and sampler schedules on top of that fixed commitment. This is an odd restriction to accept when the underlying activation family spans regimes as different as heavy-tailed, noise-dominated activations at t → T and tightly clustered structured signals at t → 0. If the family of activation distributions is the problem, the family of number formats is the natural response. Chameleon respects those architecture-specific lessons rather than discarding them: a hard fork at model load selects a UNet , few-step, or DiT execution path (Section 4.6), and because the format choice sits underneath that machinery, each path threads Chameleon’s LUT through the treatment inherited from prior work. “Which format to use” is thereby decoupled from “which architecture we are quantizing.” This is tractable because the format is a discrete variable: the palette is small (five formats for activations, six for weights at 4 bits) and fixed by what accelerators implement natively, so selection is a routing problem rather than a continuous optimization, and both statistics that decide the per-tensor Mean Squared Quantization Error ( MSQE ) comparison are cheap – kurtosis is a one-pass moment accumulation, and SNR is read off the noise schedule with no forward pass. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Extended versions of the paragraphs below, and a note on LLM quantization, are in Appendix A.1. 

Diffusion-model PTQ: Q-Diffusion (Li et al., 2023) first recognized that activation distribu-tions inside a denoising UNet are not stationary across the schedule, answering with a uniform-timestep calibration sampler and a split quantizer for the shortcut layers. PTQ4DM (Shang et al., 2023) added per-bucket calibration statistics. Both take the format itself – INT8 – as a given and tune scales around it. 

Few-step and consistency-model PTQ: Few-step distilled models – by consistency (Luo et al., 2024) or adversarial (Sauer et al., 2024) distillation – are hostile to quantization: each of their one to four steps carries far more of the semantic burden than one step of a 50-step trajectory. MixDQ (Zhao et al., 2024) combines metric-decoupled mixed precision, assigned by integer programming, with a 

BOS -aware bypass in the cross-attention K and V projections; Q-Sched (Frumkin & Marculescu, 2026), concurrent with and complementary to Chameleon, instead learns a single pair of scalar 3preconditioning coefficients that warp the sampling trajectory. Our few-step fork composes with 

MixDQ rather than replacing it (Section 4.6). 

DiT quantization: Diffusion transformers exhibit large variance across the input channel dimen-sion of their linear-layer activations, which tensor- or output-channel-wise quantization cannot ab-sorb. Q-DiT (Chen et al., 2025a) introduced group-wise weight quantization with an evolution-ary search over group sizes g ∈ { 32 , 64 , 128 , 192 , 288 }, and dynamic on-the-fly scale and zero-point computation for activations – again with INT as the only number system. ViDiT-Q (Zhao et al., 2025) reports W 8A8 and W 4A8 on PixArt -α with finer-grained dynamic quantization, again within INT . Our DiT fork keeps both, generalizing the group search to a joint (g, f ) search over group size and format (Section 4.6). PTQ4DiT (Wu et al., 2024), FP4DiT (Chen et al., 2025b) and 

ViDiT-Q are not re-run in our harness and so do not appear in Table 1 (Appendix A.1). 

Microscaling formats: The OCP MX specification (OCP Microscaling Formats Working Group, 2023; Rouhani et al., 2023) standardized block-shared-exponent narrow formats: each block of 32 elements carries one shared 8-bit exponent, absorbing intra-tensor magnitude variation without per-channel scale infrastructure. To our knowledge, Chameleon is the first PTQ scheme for diffusion models to use MX formats adaptively , routing concatenation-bearing UNet shortcut layers to MXFP8 

by topology. 

Format selection beyond INT : At a fixed bit-width the format is itself a free variable: MoFQ 

(Zhang et al., 2024) chooses INT or FP per layer for LLM s by quantization error, and So et al. (2023a) search a per-tensor floating-point encoding, layer by layer, for diffusion. TDQ (So et al., 2023b) comes closest to our temporal axis, but varies the quantization interval with t, not the format (Appendix A.1). 

Where we sit: Chameleon is the first PTQ scheme for time-stepped image generators to treat the 

number format as a discrete variable indexed by (layer, timestep bucket) – per-layer format selection at a fixed width was introduced for LLM s (Zhang et al., 2024), and timestep-dependent quantization 

parameters for diffusion (So et al., 2023b), but not the format along the schedule – and the first to demonstrate the same routing principle across UNet , few-step UNet , and image DiT under one framework. It is composable rather than competitive: where existing methods care about scales (Q-Diffusion , PTQ4DM ), bit-widths ( MixDQ ), group sizes ( Q-DiT ), or sampling trajectories (Q-Sched ), Chameleon cares about formats, and the underlying calibration loop and group search from each of these methods can sit on top of it without modification.

For each (layer, bucket) pair we apply the decision rule below, which approximates the argmin of equation 2 under a generalized-Gaussian model. UNet shortcut layers are pinned by topology before 5any statistic is consulted; the rest are routed by κ and SNR :If l is a UNet shortcut layer: f ⋆l,b = MXFP8 E4M3 (shortcut concatenation) Else if κl(t) > 5 or SNR( b) < 0.2 : f ⋆l,b = FP8 E5M2 (Phase 1, heavy tails) Else if κl(t) ≤ 3 and SNR( b) > 2 : f ⋆l,b = INT8-asym (Phase 3, clean signal) Else: f ⋆l,b = FP8 E4M3 (Phase 2, settling) Here SNR( b) ≡ SNR( tb), evaluated at the bucket midpoint tb = b T /B + T / (2 B), as in Algo-rithm 1. The SNR gate overrides kurtosis by design: below τlow every non-shortcut layer is routed to FP8 E5M2 (Table 7), so per-layer routing acts in the mid- and high-SNR buckets. The constants 

(κlow , κ high ) = (3 , 5) and (τlow , τ high ) = (0 .2, 2.0) are fixed a priori rather than tuned per model: the kurtosis gates are where the per-format MSQE curves cross under a generalized-Gaussian model, and the SNR gates mark the macroscopic phases of the noise schedule. Empirically, the routing is insensitive to their exact values: shifting the kurtosis gates from (3 .0, 5.0) to (3 .5, 5.5) – +17% and 

+10% respectively – changes FID by 0.02 , below the 0.04 run-to-run variation we observe between two independent calibrations of an identical configuration (Appendix A.9). 4.4 CALIBRATION : L OCKSTEP SAMPLING AND LUT C ONSTRUCTION 

Calibration runs as a single pass on top of a Q-Diffusion -style uniform timestep sampler. We collect 128 prompts ( COCO captions), bucket the schedule into B = 10 uniform-width windows, and generate one 20-step trajectory per prompt. During each forward pass, every Chameleon-instrumented layer accumulates running statistics into an L × B table, pooling the conditional and unconditional halves of each classifier-free-guidance batch; Algorithm 1 in the supplement gives the full pseudocode. The accumulator stores raw power sums ( n, P x, P x2, P x3, P x4) plus a running min and max, from which κl(t) follows in closed form. Memory is O(1) in the number of prompts – seven scalars per (layer, bucket) – so SDXL ’s 372 quantized layers at B = 10 need 26 ,040 scalars, about 200 KB. 4.5 PER -C HANNEL WEIGHT SELECTION

The runtime path is a single nn.Module inserted before each quantizable layer; Algorithm 2 gives the pseudocode and Appendix A.7 two reproducibility notes ( LUT buffer registration and FP8 range clamping). Here we say how our MX numbers should be read. 

MX block formats are simulated by reshaping to blocks, computing the shared exponent, round-tripping each element, and reshaping back. The activation path follows the OCP specification ex-actly: the shared scale is the power-of-two 2⌊log 2 max i |xi|⌋ (an E8M0 exponent), so the simulation matches conforming MX hardware. The SDXL weight-side implementation instead uses an uncon-strained per-block scale, which is strictly more expressive than the specification allows (the few-step path’s MX weights already use power-of-two scales, and the DiT path selects no MX weight format); the numbers we report for MX weight formats should be read accordingly. Appendix A.12 mea-sures what conformance would cost and how it changes the palette. We report fake-quant numbers throughout because real INT8 /FP8 /MX kernels are not yet uniformly available across the architec-tures we evaluate; on Blackwell we expect native kernels (e.g., Microsoft’s microxcaling ) to remove the simulation overhead. The whole framework is roughly 5.8 kLOC of PyTorch. We will release the code and the per-model 

LUT s that reproduce Section 5 exactly.

Chameleon’s calibration involves no gradient updates, no auxiliary networks, and no integer pro-gram of our own – the few-step path excepted, and only because it consumes MixDQ ’s precomputed mixed-precision assignment unchanged. Calibration takes at most 45 minutes on a single A100 ( 45 

minutes for SDXL , 7 for PixArt -α and 1 for SDXL-Turbo ) and under an hour for all three back-bones combined (Table 2 in Appendix A.7). The routing hyperparameters – the kurtosis and SNR 

thresholds and the bucket count B – are fixed across every model, with no per-model tuning; only the palette is specialized per architecture, and structurally so (Section 4.6). 

6 DISCUSSION & L IMITATIONS 

Our numbers come from fake quantization rather than real low-precision kernels, so we make no efficiency claims: they characterize what the format assignment costs in fidelity, and measuring its latency and memory savings on native FP8 and MX kernels remains future work. The evalua-tion is limited to image generation; extending the routing to video DiT s, whose temporal-attention sensitivities are uncharacterized, is a natural next step, and sub-4-bit (W 2/W 1) regimes would re-quire quantization-aware training rather than PTQ . The palette also assumes its formats exist in hardware: FP8 and MX need Hopper- or Blackwell-class support, and on INT -only NPU s it re-duces to its INT members. Per-channel mixed formats likewise do not map to one native GEMM 

tile; they would run as same-format sub-GEMM s after a channel permutation, or with weights de-quantized in registers as existing W 4A8 kernels do. Appendix A.13 expands these points, adding the per-channel format-metadata overhead at extreme model scale and calibration generalization to out-of-distribution prompts. 

7 CONCLUSION 

We argued that at a fixed bit-width the number format is not a design constant but a discrete variable, and that it should be chosen per tensor: per weight channel, per layer, and – where a timestep axis exists – per timestep bucket. The argument is structural: a format is priced for a distribution, and the distributions inside a denoising network differ across channels, across layers, and along the schedule, sliding from heavy-tailed, noise-dominated tensors at t → T to tightly clustered, structured signals at t → 0, so no single format in the standard 8-bit palette is correctly priced for all of them. Tuning scales cannot fix a format mismatch. Chameleon makes that choice adaptive. Two ahead-of-time statistics – empirical kurtosis and the closed-form diffusion SNR – populate a format lookup table that the runtime indexes with one ten-sor read, and a single architectural fork specializes the routing to UNet , few-step, and DiT back-bones. Across all three backbones and both bit-widths, the resulting quantized configurations re-duce FID below the FP16 reference while holding CLIP -score within 0.24 of it, and lead every quantized baseline on CLIP at W 4A8 (Table 1). The framework is composable rather than com-petitive: Q-DiT ’s group-wise weights and dynamic per-sample scaling consume Chameleon’s LUT 

unmodified, and MixDQ ’s activation pipeline composes with our weight palette so cleanly that its 

BOS -aware bypass is no longer required. The natural next steps – Q-Sched -style sampler adaptation, real-kernel speedups on Hopper and Blackwell, video DiT s, and sub-4-bit QAT – are independent and additive. 9REFERENCES

A.1 EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Diffusion-model PTQ: Q-Diffusion (Li et al., 2023) was the first PTQ scheme to recognize that the activation distributions inside a denoising UNet are not stationary across the diffusion sched-ule. Their fix has two parts: a uniform-timestep sampler during calibration that visits the entire schedule rather than a single timestep, and a split quantizer for the UNet ’s shortcut layers, which separately quantize the deep and shallow halves of the concatenated tensor before applying a fused matmul. PTQ4DM (Shang et al., 2023) sharpened the timestep handling by partitioning the schedule into buckets and accumulating per-bucket calibration statistics, but kept the format fixed at INT8 .Both methods take the format itself as a given and tune scales around it. Two later lines of work stay within INT but attack the error differently: PTQD (He et al., 2023) models the quantization error as correlated noise and corrects it during sampling, while EfficientDM (He et al., 2024) recovers low-bit quality by fine-tuning LoRA weights, which places it closer to QAT than to PTQ .

Few-step and consistency-model PTQ: Few-step distilled models – whether obtained by consis-tency distillation, as in Latent Consistency Models (Luo et al., 2024), or by adversarial distillation, as in SDXL-Turbo (Sauer et al., 2024) – are particularly hostile to quantization, because each of their one to four denoising steps carries a much larger fraction of the total semantic burden than a single step in a 50-step DDIM trajectory; quantization noise that an iterative model would absorb statistically becomes visible in the output. MixDQ (Zhao et al., 2024) addressed this by combining 

metric-decoupled mixed precision – separately measuring image-quality and text-alignment sen-sitivity per layer and assigning bit-widths via integer programming – with a surgical BOS -aware quantization (BAQ ) that exempts the begin-of-sentence position of the text embedding from acti-vation quantization in the cross-attention K and V projections, substituting a pre computed FP16 

output. Q-Sched (Frumkin & Marculescu, 2026) takes a different route, leaving the quantized weights frozen and learning a single pair of scalar preconditioning coefficients, applied to the latent and to the quantized noise prediction at every step, that warps the integration trajectory to compen-sate for the quantization-induced drift in the vector field. Our few-step fork applies Chameleon’s weight palette over MixDQ ’s calibrated activation pipeline, leaving the activation side entirely to 

MixDQ : with a single denoising step the timestep axis degenerates, so the routing of Section 4.6 applies to weights only; notably, its BOS -aware bypass becomes unnecessary under Chameleon’s weight palette – re-enabling a faithful equivalent changes FID by less than 0.15 (Appendix A.9) – which also lifts MixDQ ’s batch-size-one inference constraint. Q-Sched is concurrent with and complementary to Chameleon. 

DiT quantization: Diffusion transformers exhibit large variance across the input channel dimen-sion of their linear-layer activations rather than output channels, which means standard tensor or output-channel-wise quantization underflows the small-magnitude channels and crushes the model. 

Q-DiT (Chen et al., 2025a) introduced group-wise weight quantization with an evolutionary search over group sizes g ∈ { 32 , 64 , 128 , 192 , 288 }, and dynamic on-the-fly scale and zero-point computa-tion for activations because pre-calibrated scales drift unacceptably across samples. Q-DiT , again, fixes INT as the only number system. Our DiT fork keeps Q-DiT ’s group search and dynamic micro-scaling, but generalizes the search space from group-only to (g, f ) joint over group size and format choice from {INT4 , NF4 , FP4 } at 4 bits or {INT8 sym , INT8 asym , MXINT8 } at 8 bits. 

PTQ4DiT (Wu et al., 2024) instead balances channel salience to reach W 4A8 on DiT s, and FP4DiT 

(Chen et al., 2025b) quantizes PixArt -α with floating-point rather than integer formats at W 4A6.Neither they nor ViDiT-Q (Zhao et al., 2025) are re-run in our harness, so Table 1 compares against the per-architecture baseline each family established. 

Microscaling formats: The OCP MX specification (OCP Microscaling Formats Working Group, 2023; Rouhani et al., 2023) standardized a family of block-shared-exponent narrow formats – 

MXFP8 , MXINT8 , MXFP6 , MXFP4 , MXINT4 – in which each block of 32 elements carries one shared 8-bit exponent and the elements themselves are stored in a narrow integer or floating-point body. Our fake-quantization implements it directly in PyTorch (Section 4.7). The hardware payoff is that the shared exponent absorbs intra-tensor magnitude variation without any per-channel scale infrastructure. To our knowledge, Chameleon is the first PTQ scheme for diffusion models to use 

MX formats adaptively , routing concatenation-bearing UNet shortcut layers to MXFP8 by topology. 15 LLM quantization, in passing: GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023), AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) and 

SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) established the modern playbook for LLM PTQ , and 

SmoothQuant ’s idea of migrating activation outliers into the weight space inspired several dif-fusion follow-ups. Edge-deployment-oriented variants such as EntroLLM (Sanyal et al., 2026) compose entropy-coded weight compression on top of post-training quantization to cut bandwidth on resource-constrained devices. Format selection at a fixed bit-width also originates in this line: 

MoFQ (Zhang et al., 2024) chooses INT or FP per layer by quantization error for LLM s, and defers finer granularity to future work. Chameleon inherits that idea and extends it along three axes – the timestep, a palette that includes NF4 and the MX formats, and the architectural fork – none of which has a counterpart in LLM quantization, where the temporal structure Chameleon exploits is absent. A.3 ACTIVATION PHASES FOR SDXL This appendix gives an illustrative worked example of the activation statistics summarized in Sec-tion 3.2. Concretely, we instrument a fully trained SDXL UNet , run 128 calibration prompts through a 20-step denoising schedule, and record the empirical sample kurtosis 

κl(t) = E[( xl − μl)4](E[( xl − μl)2]) 2 (6) for the input activations of each of the 372 quantized convolution and linear layers – attention Q/K/V projections and the stem/head convolutions are kept in FP16 and excluded – at each of ten timestep buckets. Together with the diffusion signal-to-noise ratio SNR( t), three patterns emerge consis-tently across the instrumented layers and define the regimes Chameleon routes between. Table 7 (Appendix A.9.5) reports the per-bucket format distribution behind the percentages quoted below, and Table 3 ablates the two statistics that separate the phases. 

Phase 1 (low SNR , t → T ): κl(t) > 5 for the majority of layers. Although the network input is dominated by the additive Gaussian noise √1 − ¯αt ϵ – whose kurtosis is exactly 3 – the internal ac-tivations are markedly heavy-tailed: normalization layers and attention rescale the noise-dominated features unevenly, producing sparse large outliers. INT8 uniform quantization underperforms here because those outliers force the scale wide and the central bins go empty; the calibrated router assigns FP8 E5M2 to 97% of layers in the final bucket. 

Phase 2 (mid SNR ): As coarse structure forms, κl(t) settles into the range [3 , 5] for most layers. The distribution still has tails that INT8 handles poorly, but the dynamic range has tightened by an order of magnitude. FP8 E4M3 , with three mantissa bits and a ±448 range, is the closest match, and becomes the modal choice ( 61% of layers in the mid buckets). 16 Phase 3 (high SNR , t → 0): Activations are now dominated by the structured signal; the dis-tribution narrows toward Gaussian for the bulk of layers. INT8 , with its 256 uniform bins, is the highest-density 8-bit representation and overtakes the FP8 formats in per-format MSQE where both routing gates clear – κl ≤ 3 and SNR( t) > 2 – which occurs for roughly one layer in ten in the cleanest bucket; E4M3 remains the plurality format elsewhere. The transitions are not synchronous across layers – early-resolution convolution blocks reach Phase 3 sooner than mid-block projection layers – which is why the routing decision is made per (layer, bucket) and not per bucket alone. Every Chameleon configuration in Table 1 reports a FID below its unquantized FP16 reference. The effect spans an order of magnitude in size, from −0.34 on SDXL-Turbo at W 4A8 (21 .29 

against 21 .63 ) to −5.39 on PixArt -α at W 4A8 (22 .24 against 27 .63 ), and it appears on all three backbones at both bit-widths. The reading is unusual – one would normally expect the unquantized model to dominate – so we record what we can and cannot establish about it. 

The effect exceeds measurement noise: FID is computed on the full 24 ,576 -image generation against the 40 ,504 -image COCO -2014 reference set. Two independently calibrated runs of an iden-tical configuration differ by 0.04 FID (Appendix A.9), so inversions spanning 0.34 to 5.39 are not 21 attributable to calibration or sampling variance. Nor are they confined to a single fork: they appear under three different weight-selection procedures (per-output-channel SNR on SDXL , the adaptive palette over MixDQ activations on SDXL-Turbo , and the joint (g, f ) search on PixArt -α) and under both static and dynamic activation scaling. 

The mechanism is not uniform smoothing: A natural explanation is that aggressive rounding attenuates the very-high-frequency texture that the InceptionV3 features underlying FID over-penalize relative to perceptual quality – an effect documented in the GAN compression literature, where pruning and quantization occasionally improve FID for this reason. We tested it directly by measuring the variance of the Laplacian over 400 prompt-matched images per configuration (Table 8). The explanation holds on PixArt -α, where quantization reduces high-frequency energy from 1287 to 1057 – and that is also the backbone with the largest inversion. It does not hold for the two UNet backbones, where the quantized models carry substantially more high-frequency content than their FP16 references ( 522 → 662 on SDXL , 439 → 725 on SDXL-Turbo ). Whatever produces the inversion there, it is not a low-pass effect, and we do not have a validated account of it. 

Bottom line for the main-text claims: We report the inversions as raw measurements and make no stronger claim. Because they are measured on a single feature extractor with known frequency biases, and because we can rule out the most obvious mechanism on two of three backbones, a reader skeptical of FID as a single-number metric should treat the quantized configurations as occupying the same neighborhood as their FP16 references rather than as strictly superior. We note that the corrected CLIP scores of Appendix A.6 move in the same direction on two of three backbones – Chameleon exceeds the FP16 reference at W 4A8 on SDXL-Turbo (26 .85 against 26 .63 ) and 

PixArt -α (26 .09 against 25 .99 ) – but establishing a genuine quality improvement over FP16 

would require a perceptual metric such as human preference, which we have not run. A.9 ABLATIONS

This appendix expands Section 4.2 of the main paper by walking through each candidate format in Chameleon’s palette and identifying the kurtosis and SNR regimes (Appendix A.3) in which each is the per-format MSQE -minimizing choice. 

INT8 (asymmetric): Encodes a tensor as 256 evenly spaced bins between min and max . Bin width s = (max − min) /255 . Optimal under a uniform prior; degrades quickly under heavy tails because s inflates with the outlier magnitude. 

FP8 E4M3 : One sign bit, four exponent bits with bias 7, three mantissa bits. Representable range 

±448 , smallest normal 2−6. Mantissa precision is 2−3, so it cleanly resolves moderate dynamic range with reasonable bin density. 

FP8 E5M2 : One sign, five exponent, two mantissa. Representable range ±57344 , smallest nor-mal 2−14 . The five exponent bits give it nine orders of magnitude of dynamic range, which is what makes it the right answer in Phase 1. 

MXINT8 / MXFP8 : A microscaling block of 32 elements carries one shared 8-bit exponent (E8M0 ); the elements themselves are stored in INT8 or FP8 respectively. The shared exponent is computed per block as Eblock = ⌊log 2 max i |xi|⌋ , so a tensor containing both 10 −3 and 10 2 val-ues can be represented without either being crushed: the relevant block selects its own exponent. Appendix A.12 records where our implementation conforms to the OCP specification and what con-formance costs. This is exactly the medicine for the wide-tailed concatenations at UNet shortcuts (58 × median, up to 163 × peak-to-median channel range; Figure 3).

Why fixed thresholds survive different noise schedules: The backbones we evaluate use differ-ent β schedules – scaled-linear for SDXL and SDXL-Turbo , linear for PixArt -α – so SNR( t)

spans ∼ 10 3 down to ∼ 10 −2 on the former and ∼ 10 4 down to ∼ 10 −4 on the latter. Applying the same gates (τlow , τ high ) = (0 .2, 2.0) therefore partitions the schedule differently: four of ten buckets fall into Phase 1 on SDXL against six of ten on PixArt -α. This is the point rather than a complication. The thresholds are stated in terms of SNR( t), which each model’s own noise schedule supplies, so they adapt to the schedule without being retuned – which is what lets the hyperparame-ter footprint stay fixed across backbones (Section 5.3). The prediction is exact: the buckets the gate marks as Phase 1 on PixArt -α (b ≥ 4) are precisely those the shipped LUT fills with 100% FP8 E5M2 (Appendix A.9.5). A.12 MX C ONFORMANCE AND ITS COST 

The OCP MX specification requires the per-block shared scale to be a power of two – an E8M0 

exponent – rather than an unconstrained floating-point value. Our activation path implements this exactly: the block scale is 2⌊log 2 max i |xi|⌋ , so normalized values land in (−2, 2) and the simulation matches what conforming MX hardware computes. The SDXL weight-side implementation deviates, using an unconstrained per-block scale, which is strictly more expressive than the specification al-lows; the few-step path’s MX weights already use power-of-two scales, and the DiT path selects no 

MX weight format at all. We measured the cost of conformance directly by rounding the weight-side scale to the nearest power of two on real SDXL weights. It is 3.5 dB of SQNR – 45 .4 → 41 .9 for MXINT8 and 20 .3 →

16 .7 for MXINT4 – close to the theoretical worst case of 6 dB from forfeiting up to one bit of range. The consequence for the palette is larger than that figure suggests. Under an unconstrained scale, block-scaled MXINT8 wins essentially every output channel at 8 bits, which would make the weight palette degenerate; under E8M0 the per-channel selection of Section 4.5 distributes across 

INT8-asym (59% ), MXINT8 (37% ) and INT8-sym (4% ). Conformance is therefore what makes the per-channel search meaningful at 8 bits rather than a formality. The SDXL weight-side numbers reported in this paper correspond to the unconstrained variant and should be read as an upper bound on what a conforming MX weight kernel would deliver; the activation-side numbers are conformant as reported. A.13 EXTENDED DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Real hardware versus fake-quant: All numbers reported in Section 5 use fake-quantization – round-trip casts inside FP16 – because real INT4 /NF4 /FP4 /MX4 kernels are not yet uniformly available across the three backbones we evaluate. We therefore make no efficiency claims: the results characterize what the format assignment costs in fidelity, not what it saves in latency or memory. Establishing the latter would require native FP8 and MX kernels – MX being a Blackwell-class hardware feature, with FP8 already native on Hopper and Ada – and we leave a real-kernel measurement to a hardware-focused follow-up. 27 Composition with sampler adaptation: Q-Sched (Frumkin & Marculescu, 2026) keeps the quantized weights frozen and learns two scalar preconditioning coefficients per sampler step that warp the integration trajectory. Their motivation – that aggressive quantization changes the un-derlying vector field, so the sampler optimized for FP16 is no longer optimal – is orthogonal to Chameleon’s per-bucket format choice. Composing the two should be straightforward: run Chameleon’s calibration first to fix the format LUT , then run Q-Sched ’s preconditioning search on top of the now-quantized weights. We have not measured this composition and leave it to future work. 

Image scope: The current evaluation is limited to image generation. We expect the same routing principle to carry over to video diffusion transformers – the macro physics of the diffusion schedule, and therefore the kurtosis/ SNR phase structure that Chameleon exploits, is unchanged in the tempo-ral setting – but the temporal-attention layers in video DiT s introduce additional sensitivity that we have not yet characterized. Extending Chameleon to video DiT s is a natural next step. 

Sub-4-bit territory: We do not address W 2 or W 1 quantization. At those bit-widths the dis-crete approximation error overwhelms what any post-training routing scheme can recover, and quantization-aware training with techniques such as BinaryDM ’s evolvable-basis binarizer (Zheng et al., 2025) becomes necessary. Chameleon’s calibration pass could in principle initialize such a 

QAT phase, but doing so is outside the scope of a PTQ paper. 

Large per-channel format metadata: At extreme model scale (e.g. a hypothetical 20-billion-parameter image DiT ) the per-output-channel format byte starts to add measurable overhead. We expect that a per-layer format choice with per-channel scale would recover most of the benefit at a fraction of the metadata cost, since within a single conv layer the dominant format choice tends to be shared across most output channels. 

Calibration on out-of-distribution prompts: Our calibration uses 128 COCO captions for all three runs. We have not stress-tested generalization to dramatically different prompt distributions – for instance, calibrating on COCO and evaluating on text-rendering or hands-and-faces bench-marks. A practitioner deploying Chameleon on a domain-specific generator should re-calibrate with a domain-matched prompt set. 28 B STATEMENTS

This work studies post-training quantization of existing, publicly released text-to-image diffusion models. It involves no human subjects, no personally identifying information, and no new data collection: all evaluation uses the public COCO -2014 validation set (Lin et al., 2014) and publicly released model checkpoints under their respective licenses. Our method inherits the characteristics of the models it compresses. Text-to-image diffusion models are known to reproduce social biases present in their training data and can be used to generate misleading or harmful imagery. Quantization does not introduce these properties, but neither does it remove them, and we did not evaluate whether format routing shifts a model’s behavior on safety-relevant prompts – our evaluation measures distributional fidelity and prompt adherence only. A practitioner deploying a quantized model should not assume that safety evaluations performed on the full-precision model transfer unchanged. The intended benefit of this work is to reduce the memory and compute required to run these models, which lowers the barrier to on-device inference and reduces the energy cost of deployment. We note the dual-use character of that benefit: the same reduction makes generative models easier to deploy for misuse, and easier to run outside the monitoring that a hosted service provides. We judge the efficiency and accessibility benefits to outweigh this risk for a method that adds no capability beyond what the underlying models already have. B.3 REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT
