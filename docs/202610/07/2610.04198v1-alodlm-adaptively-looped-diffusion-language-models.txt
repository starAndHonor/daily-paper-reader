ABSTRACT 

Diffusion language models (DLMs) enable fast generation by predicting multiple tokens in parallel, yet their practical adoption remains hindered by a persistent quality gap relative to comparably sized autoregressive (AR) models. We attribute this gap to a computation–difficulty mismatch : within a partially observed sequence, some unknown tokens are readily predictable, while others require substantially more computation to resolve. Existing DLMs, however, apply uniform compu-tational depth to every unknown position at each denoising step. We introduce 

ALoDLM , which replaces this uniform computation with token-adaptive latent recurrence. At each denoising step, ALoDLM iteratively refines representations in latent space, allocating computation based on token difficulty. Tokens ready to commit are fed back as discrete context, while unresolved tokens retain and refine their latent states through additional recurrent passes. To learn both token prediction and computation allocation end-to-end, we formulate token-wise com-putation schedules as latent variables and derive a conditional negative evidence lower bound (NELBO). We train ALoDLM at 1.7B and 8B parameter scales. Across eleven benchmarks, ALoDLM outperforms all evaluated DLMs and the correspond-ing AR baselines in average benchmark score at both scales. Importantly, ALoDLM

1arXiv:2610.04198v1 [cs.AI] 3 Oct 2026 1 INTRODUCTION 

Autoregressive (AR) large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across diverse tasks (Radford et al., 2018; Raffel et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2020). However, their sequential, token-by-token decoding imposes a severe latency bottleneck on generation (Leviathan et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024). Diffusion language models (DLMs) (Austin et al., 2021a; Lou et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024; Ou et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025) offer a promising alternative: they iteratively denoise corrupted sequences, resolving multiple masked positions in parallel to enable substantially faster generation (Zhang et al., 2025). Despite this potential, their practical adoption remains hindered by a persistent quality gap relative to comparably sized AR models. Existing approaches mitigate this gap either by reintroducing autoregressive structures via semi-AR designs like block diffusion (Arriola et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a; Cheng et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Fu et al., 2026), or by relegating DLMs to drafting candidates for subsequent AR verification (Liu et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2026a; Yu et al., 2026; Sahoo et al., 2026). We instead ask a more fundamental question: 

What is the root cause underlying the generation quality gap in DLMs? 

We hypothesize that a key bottleneck in DLM’s generation quality is a computation–difficulty mismatch . During a single denoising step, masked positions can vary significantly in prediction difficulty due to intrinsic uncertainty (H¨ ullermeier & Waegeman, 2021), multihop reasoning require-ments (Huang et al., 2025), or dependencies on unresolved tokens (Wu et al., 2025b). Despite this variation, standard DLMs apply the same fixed-depth denoiser to all masked positions, training each to predict its target directly from a corrupted context. This uniform allocation wastes computation on easy predictions while starving difficult ones of necessary compute. AR generation largely avoids this within-step allocation problem by predicting a single next token conditioned on a fully resolved prefix. Confidence-based decoding (Nie et al., 2025) partially alleviates the problem by deferring low-confidence predictions to later denoising steps. However, the deferred tokens ultimately receive the same fixed compute since the latent computation performed for an unresolved token is discarded, and the token is recomputed from its mask embedding at the next step. This limitation motivates our central principle: DLMs should maintain and refine persistent latent states for challenging tokens within each denoising step, allowing computation to scale dynamically with prediction difficulty. In this work, we propose ALoDLM , a novel class of DLMs designed to overcome this bottleneck by adaptively allocating computation across tokens during mask recovery. Our first contribution is a looped architecture with token-adaptive recurrent depth. In this design, easy tokens commit early to provide resolved context, while difficult tokens undergo additional recurrent passes to continually refine their persistent latent states. Building on this architecture, we formulate the token-wise computation schedule as a latent variable and derive a unified objective that jointly optimizes the base language model and the compute-allocation policy. Finally, we derive an unbiased stochastic gradient estimator equipped with variance reduction techniques for stable end-to-end training. Experimental results demonstrate that ALoDLM achieves state-of-the-art generation quality while maintaining fast parallel decoding. We train ALoDLM at 1.7B and 8B scales through direct SFT of Qwen3 backbones on a 5B-token corpus. In quality evaluations across eleven benchmarks spanning general reasoning, mathematics, and coding, ALoDLM achieves average scores of 65.5 and 80.3, surpassing all evaluated DLMs and the corresponding AR baselines. Furthermore, with parallel decoding on GSM8K, ALoDLM -8B delivers approximately 2.7× the speed of vLLM-served Qwen3-8B at comparable or higher accuracy, demonstrating a strong quality–efficiency trade-off. Finally, our analyses show that recurrent depth provides an effective test-time scaling axis and that ALoDLM learns token-dependent refinement preferences without explicit difficulty labels. Our main contributions are: • Token-adaptive looped architecture. We introduce a looped architecture with dynamic recurrent depth. Easy tokens commit early to act as resolved context, while difficult tokens continue to refine their accumulated latent states through additional recurrent passes. • Principled training objective. We formulate token-wise exit schedules as latent variables and derive a conditional ELBO to jointly optimize token prediction and computation allocation. To stabilize training, we develop an unbiased single-trajectory gradient estimator coupled with effective variance reduction techniques. 2• Scaling and performance. We scale ALoDLM to 8B parameters, making it to the best of our knowledge, the largest looped DLM to date. It achieves state-of-the-art average benchmark performance among all evaluated DLMs and outperforms its Qwen3-based AR counterparts, all while maintaining fast parallel generation. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Looped Transformers. Looped (or recurrent-depth) transformers repeatedly apply layers or blocks of layers, effectively increasing network depth without expanding the parameter count (Dehghani et al., 2019; Xue et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2025b; Bae et al., 2025; Nanbeige Lab et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026; Jeddi et al., 2026; Movahedi et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026b). This looping mechanism serves as a new scaling axis, yielding performance gains on both algorithmic (Giannou et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024) and reasoning tasks (Saunshi et al., 2025; Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025b; Huang et al., 2026). A related line of research leverages this recurrence for adaptive computation ,where the primary challenge is learning a policy to determine when to halt. ACT (Graves, 2016) and Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019) learn halting through continuous weighting of intermediate states or outputs. PonderNet (Banino et al., 2021) and Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025a) instead optimize the expected prediction loss by explicitly summing over candidate exit depths, weighted by learned halting probabilities. In ALoDLM , token commitments change the context of unresolved positions, making exact summation over joint exit schedules intractable. 

Looped Diffusion Language Models. Diffusion language models (DLMs) generate text through parallel token denoising and are typically trained with an ELBO-derived cross-entropy objective over masked positions (Sahoo et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024; Ou et al., 2024). Recent work has integrated looping mechanisms into DLMs to improve training efficiency (Lee et al., 2026), parameter efficiency (Carballo-Castro et al., 2026), and step-wise adaptive computation (Miele et al., 2026). Crucially, they apply a uniform recurrent depth across all token positions within each denoising step, lacking the flexibility to let individual tokens exit latent refinement adaptively. While FReDA (Sun et al., 2026a) supports token-level adaptive commitment, it iteratively revises an explicit token draft rather than propagating an internal latent state across loops. In contrast, ALoDLM enables token-wise adaptive latent depth: easy tokens commit at earlier inner passes to provide their embeddings as context, while harder positions retain their evolving hidden states for continued refinement. For other related topics such as self-conditioning , we refer the reader to Appendix A for an extended discussion. 

3 METHOD 

We introduce ALoDLM , a family of DLMs with token-adaptive recurrent computation. We first describe its inference procedure in §3.1, then derive the training objective and present techniques for gradient estimation and variance reduction in §3.2. 3.1 INFERENCE 

Overview. At each denoising step (the outer loop ), a conventional fixed-depth DLM predicts masked tokens in parallel using the same denoiser depth. ALoDLM augments each step with an inner loop that allows tokens to commit at different recurrent depths. Committed tokens provide discrete context for subsequent predictions, while unresolved positions preserve their latent states for further refinement. Predictive confidence controls token commitment, and a learned halting policy determines when to end the inner loop. Algorithm 1 and Figure 2 summarize the procedure.

Training ALoDLM involves jointly learning a de-noiser and a halting policy that dictates when to commit predictions. This section first intro-duces the joint exit schedule (Definition 3.1) and its effect on token predictions. We then derive a variational objective to optimize both components (Eq. (5)). Finally, we present an unbiased gradient estimator to optimize this objective (Proposition 1) and introduce practical regularization and variance reduction techniques.

Training details. We convert Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) into ALoDLM -1.7B and 

ALoDLM -8B, respectively, using WeDLM’s streaming block-diffusion framework (Liu et al., 2025a), which enables full-sequence conditioning with a standard causal attention mask. Both models use a maximum recurrent depth of K = 4 . The recurrent core comprises all 28 Transformer layers at 1.7B and the middle 16 layers at 8B. Unlike prior conversion pipelines combining continued pretraining (CPT) and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) (Tian et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Cheng et al., 2025), we skip CPT and directly perform SFT on a 5B-token corpus using AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with learning rate 10 −5 and weight decay 0.01 . We defer the full details to Appendix D. 

Benchmarks. We evaluate general knowledge and scientific reasoning on ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a), MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), and GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023); mathematical reasoning on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021b); and code generation on HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), sanitized MBPP (Austin et al., 2021b), and their EvalPlus variants (Liu et al., 2023). We report accuracy or exact match for non-code tasks, and execution-based pass@1 for coding benchmarks. Fol-lowing SDAR (Cheng et al., 2025), we adopt the OpenCompass evaluation protocol (OpenCompass Contributors, 2023), utilizing each model’s native chat template, greedy decoding, and a 4,096-token generation limit across all tasks. Test-time scaling. Figure 4 (left) shows that, after the initial training phase, deeper recurrent readouts in ALoDLM -8B exhibit lower training loss on their active tokens, consistent with progressive refinement. At inference time, increasing the exit threshold q raises the average number of loops per token from 1.6 to 2.34 and the average score across eleven benchmarks from 77 .9% to 79 .1% 

(Figure 4, middle). These results validate q as a control for average recurrent depth and demonstrate the effectiveness of latent recurrence as an axis for test-time scaling. 

Token-adaptive recurrence. Figure 4 (right) compares mean first-pass halt probabilities across token types in ALoDLM -8B, averaged equally over GSM8K, MATH-500, MBPP-sanitized, and HumanEval. Numerical tokens have the lowest mean halt probability ( 0.369 ), approximately 11 .5% below the cross-dataset reference mean ( 0.417 ), while word tokens have the highest ( 0.428 ). This lower halting propensity indicates a stronger learned preference for further latent refinement at numerical positions. Such a preference is consistent with the importance of precise numerical predictions in mathematical reasoning and code generation, where small errors in values can alter the final result. These observations support our approach to mitigating the computation–difficulty mismatch :learned token-dependent halting preferences can guide additional computation toward predictions requiring further refinement. This adaptive behavior emerges from jointly learning token prediction and the halting policy, without explicit difficulty labels or predefined halting targets for different token categories. 

Maximum recurrent depth. Figure 5 (left) compares ALoDLM -8B trained with K ∈ { 2, 4, 8} under matched settings, reporting the mean score across six benchmarks. At matched training steps, K = 2 

and K = 4 improve faster initially than K = 8 , but K = 2 plateaus at a lower score. With half the maximum recurrent depth, K = 4 achieves comparable performance to K = 8 later in training, motivating our choice of K = 4 .

Loop placement. Prior looped models vary in the extent and placement of recurrence (Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025a; Lee et al., 2026). Figure 5 (middle) compares recurrence over the middle layers [10 , 26) and last layers [20 , 36) of Qwen3-8B, with the full-stack trajectory [0 , 36) included as a historical reference. Middle-layer recurrence outperforms last-layer recurrence later in training, 9although both cores contain 16 layers. This result suggests that effective latent refinement depends on where recurrence is applied, motivating the middle-layer core of ALoDLM -8B. 

Variance reduction. Figure 5 (right) evaluates the effect of intermediate denoiser supervision on gradient noise in ALoDLM -8B. Using fixed corrupted inputs and paired exit trajectories, we compare weighted supervision over pre-commitment readouts with supervision at the sampled exit alone. For the measured subset of 4,096 denoiser normalization parameters, removing intermediate supervision increases conditional gradient variance to 1.76 ×, 1.49 ×, and 1.42 × the full-method value at training steps 1,000, 6,500, and 17,000, respectively. These results justify reusing intermediate predictions to reduce noise from exit-trajectory sampling, providing a less noisy denoiser training signal for latent refinement without additional recurrent forward passes. 

5 LIMITATIONS 

First, ALoDLM can exhibit a longer Time to First Token than a comparable AR model, as the KV-cache is constructed for the full recurrent depth during the prefill phase. This computational overhead can diminish the benefits of parallel decoding for short responses or latency-sensitive applications. Second, generation speed is inherently input-dependent. Because the amount of recurrent computation and the number of tokens committed simultaneously rely on token confidence and adaptive halting decisions, throughput can vary significantly across prompts, datasets, and domains—even under greedy decoding. Consequently, on domains underrepresented in the training data, the model may require additional recurrent refinement and make fewer parallel commitments, potentially reducing or entirely reversing its speed advantage over an optimized AR baseline. 

6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced ALoDLM , a family of adaptively looped diffusion language models that address the 

computation–difficulty mismatch through token-adaptive latent recurrence. Within each denoising step, confident tokens commit early to provide discrete context, while unresolved tokens retain and refine their latent states across recurrent passes. We derived a conditional ELBO over latent token-wise exit schedules to guide computation allocation through sequence-level credit assignment, and incorporated intermediate supervision to support denoiser training. Across eleven benchmarks, 

ALoDLM outperforms all evaluated diffusion models and the corresponding Qwen3 baselines in aver-age score at both 1.7B and 8B scales. With optimized inference, ALoDLM -8B delivers approximately 

2.7× the throughput of vLLM-served Qwen3-8B at comparable accuracy on GSM8K. AI USE STATEMENT

16 A ADDITIONAL RELATED WORK 

Discrete Diffusion Language Models generally fall into two families based on their corruption processes: masked DLMs and uniform DLMs (Austin et al., 2021a). During the reverse process, standard masked diffusion fixes tokens once unmasked, whereas uniform diffusion allows for con-tinuous revision across subsequent denoising steps. While most established DLM rely on masked diffusion (Sahoo et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025; Arriola et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025a; Lee et al., 2026; Carballo-Castro et al., 2026; Hersche et al., 2026; Miele et al., 2026), recent successes like DiffusionGemma and Duo highlight the growing viability of uniform DLMs (Team et al., 2026; Sahoo et al., 2025). However, despite their parallel generation capabilities, masked DLMs continue to face a quality gap when compared to strong autoregressive (AR) models (Arriola et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025b). To bridge this gap, recent work mainly focuses on two trajectories. The first line of research reintroduces AR structure into the diffusion process. For example, Block Diffusion generates sequences in autoregressive blocks while denoising tokens within each block in parallel (Arriola et al., 2025)—a blockwise formulation that Fast-dLLM v2 (Wu et al., 2025a) and SDAR (Cheng et al., 2025) extend to pretrained AR models. Other approaches integrate AR and diffusion more deeply: WeDLM (Liu et al., 2025a) employs topological reordering to merge parallel mask recovery with standard causal attention and streaming prefix commitment, while Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion (Fu et al., 2026) jointly trains on AR and block-diffusion objectives to support standard generation, diffusion, and self-speculative decoding within a single architecture. A second line of research exploits diffusion’s parallelism to draft candidates for subsequent AR verification. In single-pass architectures, TiDAR (Liu et al., 2025b) executes diffusion drafting and AR verification simultaneously, while I-DLM (Yu et al., 2026) uses introspective consistency training and strided decoding to verify past proposals while generating new ones concurrently. Other methods decouple the drafting process: DFlash (Chen et al., 2026a) employs a separate, lightweight block-diffusion drafter conditioned on the target AR model’s context features, whereas Uno (Sahoo et al., 2026) learns parallel diffusion adapters atop frozen AR weights, utilizing rejection sampling against the AR distribution to achieve lossless acceleration. 

Looped Transformers reuse shared architectural blocks to increase effective depth without expand-ing their parameter count, enabling strong algorithmic computation and parameter-efficient in-context learning (Giannou et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Saunshi et al., 2025). Recent architectural advances have enriched this paradigm by integrating recurrence with mixture-of-experts (Xue et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2026), efficient caching mechanisms (Sun et al., 2026b), agentic post-training (Nanbeige Lab et al., 2026), and variable-depth latent reasoning (Geiping et al., 2025; Jeddi et al., 2026). These mod-els also exhibit unique scaling properties: virtual-depth scaling enhances reasoning capabilities with minimal impact on knowledge capacity (Zhu et al., 2025b), while SMELT demonstrates significant compute-efficiency gains for looped MoE architectures under matched resource budgets (Wang et al., 2026). To further optimize these models, Parcae stabilizes recurrence and establishes training scaling laws that jointly optimize loops and data, alongside a saturating inference scaling law (Prairie et al., 2026). Additionally, Chen et al. (2026c) demonstrate that depth growth and boundary operators can improve scaling exponents, with static looping serving as an effective regularizer for multi-epoch training on limited data. To support adaptive computation within these loops, models such as ACT and Universal Transformers learn halting mechanisms using weighted intermediate states or out-puts (Graves, 2016; Dehghani et al., 2019), whereas Mixture-of-Recursions dynamically routes tokens to varying recurrent depths (Bae et al., 2025). Other approaches focus on stabilizing deep recurrence through fixed-point reasoners (Movahedi et al., 2026) or by learning solution attractors (Huang et al., 2026). To train these adaptive halting mechanisms, frameworks like PonderNet and Ouro explicitly sum prediction losses across candidate exit depths using learned halting probabilities (Banino et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2025a). However, this approach presents a critical challenge for ALoDLM : because early token commitments inherently alter the context for subsequent, unresolved positions, computing an exact summation over all joint commitment schedules becomes computationally intractable. 

Self-conditioning is a technique that improves diffusion sample quality by feeding the denoiser its own previous clean-data estimate as an auxiliary input. Originally introduced in Analog Bits (Chen et al., 2023), the standard training recipe constructs this input via an additional forward pass and applies a stop-gradient to the resulting estimate. During sampling, the estimate from the preceding denoising step is reused, creating a recursive feedback loop that indirectly propagates information from earlier steps. Subsequent work has adapted this paradigm in various ways. Simple Self-Conditioning (Cardei et al., 2026) applies detached prediction feedback directly to masked diffusion, 17 whereas DiffusionGemma (Team et al., 2026) maps predicted probabilities into continuous condi-tioning features. Other related approaches modify the feedback mechanism itself: Soft-Masked DLMs (Hersche et al., 2026) blend mask embeddings with confidence-weighted top-k token embed-dings, while DMax (Chen et al., 2026b) pairs soft embedding feedback with on-policy training. State reuse has also been extended beyond pure predictions. For instance, Loopholing (Jo et al., 2026) propagates hidden states across denoising steps using a two-pass self-conditioning training scheme, and CoFRe (Miele et al., 2026) reuses latent states to initialize successive fixed-point solvers.In contrast to conventional self-conditioning methods that rely on detached prediction feedback across denoising steps, ALoDLM maintains persistent hidden states across recurrent passes within each denoising step. By backpropagating through these recurrent passes, ALoDLM explicitly trains earlier states to support future predictions, utilizing a conditional NELBO to jointly learn both the denoiser and a token-wise halting policy.

The fallback and shared coda buffers are serving approximations: their outputs need not match those obtained by recomputing every prefix token at every depth. Related early-exit and recurrent models face the same need to provide attention context when computation depths differ (Elbayad et al., 2020; Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025a). B.2 ACCOUNTING FOR RECURRENT COMPUTATION 

Layer evaluations clarify the cost of a middle-layer recurrent core. With S executed passes, the prelude runs once, whereas both the core and the coda readout run at every pass: 

Clayers (S) = Lstart + S(Lend − Lstart ) + S(L − Lend ). (11) 18 For the 8B partition [10 , 26) , the full four-pass path evaluates 10 + 4 · 16 + 4 · 10 = 114 Transformer layers, compared with 4 · 36 = 144 for four full-stack passes. The 1.7B configuration repeats all 28 layers, giving 28 S evaluations. These are per-position layer counts for a dense execution path, not FLOP counts per generated token. A window with unresolved positions can be processed several times, and a pass can commit several tokens. Moreover, positions already committed within the window can still incur computation as context. With batching, the shared execution can run to the largest stopping depth among the active sequences. We therefore measure serving cost from executed operations and generated-token counts, rather than inferring speed from the distribution of token commitment depths alone.

Training examples are formatted as conversations, packed into 4,096-token sequences, and supervised on the completion tokens; prompt tokens provide context. Each 32-token block receives an independently sampled masking probability in [0 , 1] , and the denoiser losses use inverse-mask-probability weights normalized over the local minibatch’s supervised masked positions. The outcome actor uses sequence-summed costs with one masked-token-count normalization, as specified in Appendix C.4. The training recipe also includes a next-token prediction auxiliary loss with the same relative weight as the diffusion loss. 

Optimization. Table 2 lists the settings shared across the two scales. Both use AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) and DeepSpeed ZeRO-2. The 8B schedule runs for eight epochs (34,512 optimizer steps) and uses activation checkpointing on every second recurrent-layer invocation; the 1.7B configuration does not require it. For latent-depth training, each rollout samples commitments from the learned gate and feeds the committed target embeddings into later passes. Generation quality. The benchmark comparison in Table 1 follows the OpenCompass proto-col (OpenCompass Contributors, 2023) adopted by SDAR (Cheng et al., 2025). We apply each model’s native chat template, use greedy token selection, and cap generation at 4,096 tokens. ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, MMLU, MMLU-Pro, and GPQA-Diamond measure general and scientific reasoning; GSM8K and MATH-500 measure mathematical reasoning; HumanEval, sanitized MBPP, and their EvalPlus variants measure code generation. We report accuracy or exact match for non-code benchmarks and execution-based pass@1 for code. The table’s average is the unweighted mean of its eleven benchmark scores. For this quality comparison, each model pass commits one token using each method’s default decoding rule. ALoDLM uses a window size of 16 and evaluates the mean cumulative halting criterion on the 23 remaining positions, with q = 0 .4 at 1.7B and q = 0 .5 at 8B. This preset disables entropy-based selection, so τ is inactive; a single denoising step can still commit multiple tokens over successive recurrent passes. Diffusion baselines use their single-token presets, including uncertainty-based position selection where supported. 

Inference efficiency. Figures 1 and 3 use parallel commitment and single-stream generation on one NVIDIA B200. ALoDLM and WeDLM use their native CUDA-graph engines; Qwen3 uses vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023). SDAR uses JetEngine, LLaDA uses dInfer (Ma et al., 2025), Fast-dLLM-v2 uses its native engine, and Dream uses a compiled Fast-dLLM-v1 implementation with prefix caching (Wu et al., 2025b). Generation timing includes prefill and decoding, with device synchronization, and excludes input tokenization and answer scoring. Dataset throughput is the total generated-token count divided by the summed generation time, rather than the mean of per-example rates. The q, τ sweeps use a 16-token window and position coefficient γ = 0 .02 ; the quality preset above and this entropy-based parallel decoder are separate operating modes.
