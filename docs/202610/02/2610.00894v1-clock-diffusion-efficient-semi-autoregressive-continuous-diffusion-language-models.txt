## ABSTRACT 

Recent works on continuous diffusion for discrete data have demonstrated per-formance on par with comparable discrete diffusion models. However, these continuous counterparts lack key features that are essential to practical use as language models, namely variable-length generation and support for a key-value cache, and they still lag behind the frontier of autoregressive and discrete diffusion quality. In this work, we address these limitations. We do so by introducing a model parameterization that uses position-dependent noise schedules to define semi-autoregressive (SAR) continuous diffusion language models (DLMs). To-gether with efficient training and sampling algorithms, we call this framework 

Clock Diffusion , and we present two special cases of our method: block and slid-ing window generation. We then define ClockDLMs , a family of Gaussian DLMs based on sliding window Clock Diffusion that attain state-of-the-art diffusion like-lihood bounds on OpenWebText, even beating the performant block SAR discrete diffusion models. ClockDLMs trained on TinyGSM also substantially outperform continuous baselines on the GSM8K benchmark and match and exceed comparable SAR discrete diffusion models. Finally, building on our parameterization, we propose more efficient samplers that we dub Cache Grab , which adapt techniques from accelerated inference in discrete diffusion, such as committing tokens whose probabilities exceed a confidence threshold and self-speculative decoding, further improving our models’ quality and efficiency.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Efforts to adapt diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Song et al., 2019; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2020) to discrete domains, such as text, can be split into two streams of work. The first relies on embedding the data in a continuous representation and then applying standard continuous diffusion on this space (Li et al., 2022; Gulrajani et al., 2023). The second and historically more successful approach thus far has been to directly define the corruption and denoising processes on the discrete representations (Austin et al., 2021; Campbell et al., 2022; Lou et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2024a; Schiff et al., 2024). A recent resurgence of continuous models has challenged this paradigm (Chen et al., 2026; Davis et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026; Potaptchik et al., 2026; Roos et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026), showing competitive performance with full sequence discrete diffusion. However, these latest full-sequence continuous models cannot be used in practice, as they do not support variable-length generation with KV caching. Moreover, their performance lags behind autoregressive (AR) language models (LMs) and semi-autoregressive (SAR) discrete diffusion. In this work, we therefore present SAR continuous diffusion language models (DLMs), unlocking arbitrary length generation and KV caching for continuous models while substantially improving quality. We propose a framework consisting of variable noise schedules along the sequence (Ruhe et al., 2024) and efficient training and sampling algorithms that we call Clock Diffusion , owing to our use of position-dependent local clocks that reparameterize the global time of the diffusion process. We use our parameterization to formulate block and sliding window SAR generation. Combining Gaussian diffusion over token embeddings with sliding window Clock Diffusion, we introduce ClockDLMs. We train and evaluate these models on widely used language datasets. Our model is the first continuous DLM to match block discrete diffusion on perplexity and sets a new state of the art among continuous models. For the sliding window model, our sampling algorithm enables KV cache updates at every iteration. Building on this, we propose accelerated inference methods that we dub Cache Grab samplers, which adapt techniques from discrete diffusion, such as committing tokens with probabilities above a confidence threshold (Wu et al., 2025) and self-speculative decoding (Fu et al., 2026). 

Contributions In summary, our contributions are as follows: 1) We introduce Clock Diffusion, a framework for variable-length generation and KV caching for continuous DLMs that consists of a probabilistic model and efficient training and sampling algorithms. 2) We define ClockDLMs, a new family of Gaussian DLMs based on sliding window Clock Diffusion. 3) We develop samplers, which accelerate inference with ClockDLMs by adapting successful methods from discrete diffusion. We name these Cache Grab samplers. 4) ClockDLMs achieve SoTA among all continuous DLMs on language modeling (as measured by PPL on OWT), even beating discrete block DLMs. When combined with our Cache Grab samplers, we also achieve the highest GSM8K accuracy among comparably sized models trained on TinyGSM.

Building on our framework, we combine Sliding Window Clock Diffusion with Gaussian noise on token embeddings , i.e., we use a learnable embedding matrix E ∈ RV ×d as the embedder and perform diffusion over clean embeddings e := E⊤x, and we parameterize our denoising model as

Together these elements form a new continuous DLM family that we call Clock Diffusion Language Models ( ClockDLMs ). As an ablation, below, we also train a block version, B-ClockDLMs. See Appendices B and C for full details. 3.6 CACHE GRAB SAMPLERS : A CCELERATED INFERENCE FOR CLOCK DLM S

In our sampling algorithm, any tokens whose local clocks transition to uℓ(s) = 0 are committed and added to the cache (lines 9–11 of Algorithm 1). Here we introduce samplers that enlarge this set by optionally grabbing additional token positions for the cache. These Cache Grab samplers can thus accelerate ClockDLM inference by skipping function evaluations for early-committed tokens. 

Cache Grab-Confidence In discrete diffusion, several works accelerate generation with little to no cost in quality by utilizing heuristics to select which token positions to generate. For example, Wu et al. (2025) propose to greedily unmask all positions for which the softmax output associated with the arg max token exceeds some threshold. We can adapt this heuristic for ClockDLMs. Specifically, after every denoising step, we examine the probability associated with the arg max token at every position, and we early commit any contiguous left-to-right positions that exceed our desired threshold, saving any remaining function evaluations in these tokens’ denoising budgets. The accepted tokens can then be added to the KV cache and all remaining tokens from the active window shift left, continuing their denoising progress from where they left off, as new noisy tokens are added to the rightmost slots of the window. This sampler is depicted in Figure 3 and Algorithm 5 in Appendix E.1. In Appendix E.1, we present an adapted version of this sampler for B-ClockDLMs. 

Cache Grab-Self-Speculative Decoding Notably, our sliding window attention mask coincides with the one proposed in hybrid AR + diffusion training (Gat et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026). In particular, Fu et al. (2026) use this mask to enable a ‘self-speculative decoding’ (SSD) mode, a variant of speculative decoding (Chen et al., 2023; Leviathan et al., 2023) where the diffusion drafter and AR verifier share all parameters. Inspired by this work, we similarly propose a Cache Grab-SSD sampler. As in Fu et al. (2026), we require a jointly trained AR prediction on the first half of the 2L training sequences, and at inference, we perform two function evaluations, one in diffusion mode to produce token drafts, and one in AR mode to verify and accept a prefix of the active window. Unlike the linear self-speculative sampler of Fu et al. (2026), which restarts drafting from all mask tokens, Cache Grab-SSD retains the noisy states and denoising progress of uncommitted positions. Our work is most related to the recent continuous DLMs that revived token embedding diffusion with modernized archi-tectures and training recipes (Chen et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). We also leverage vectorized training methods intro-duced in block discrete diffusion (Arriola et al., 2025a). Han et al. (2022) used SAR decoding with diffusion over logits, but lagged in quality relative to discrete diffusion and did not propose our efficient training and KV cache sampling. 

Position-dependent Noise Schedules Previous diffusion works have explored position-dependent noise. AR Diffusion (Wu et al., 2023) biased noise across the full sequence to have leftmost tokens be less noisy, and Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024) generalized this method. Most closely related is Rolling Diffusion (Ruhe et al., 2024), which formalized a process akin to our Sliding Window Clock Diffusion. However, unlike these works, we propose efficient vectorized training and KV cache sampling. Furthermore, we complement our sampling algorithm with the Cache Grab samplers, which significantly boost throughput. Finally, both Chen et al. (2024) and Ruhe et al. (2024) examined continuous data, such as video frames, while we have adapted this method to language and derived each component of the variational bound in this setting. 

## 6 DISCUSSION & C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduced a semi-autoregressive formulation for continuous diffusion language modeling that relies on position-dependent noise schedules that determine which tokens are actively being denoised at any given diffusion step. We complement this parameterization with efficient training recipes and KV cache sampling and call this framework Clock Diffusion. We demonstrated two instantiations of Clock Diffusion that enable block and sliding window SAR generation. Addi-tionally, we leveraged the sliding window formulation to develop accelerated sampling techniques that adapt confidence-threshold and self-speculative decoding from discrete diffusion. We intro-duced models that use Clock Diffusion with Gaussian noise on token embeddings, ClockDLMs, and trained them on unconditional and conditional language modeling tasks. We attained SoTA language modeling performance measured in both perplexity and downstream benchmark metrics. We consis-tently outperformed full sequence continuous language models on both performance and efficiency, demonstrating the usefulness of our SAR parameterization and KV cache enabled sampling. 

Limitations The improved language modeling performance of our method comes at the cost of decoding flexibility, which may be beneficial in certain domains that lack a more strict left-to-right bias. Additionally, we have presently only explored relatively smaller scale models. 

Future Directions In future efforts, we intend to address the limitations of our work with extensions to any-order sliding window implementations. We also plan to test our method at larger scales and explore the efficacy of leveraging pre-trained AR and discrete diffusion models. Finally, we intend to experiment with hybrid discrete and continuous parameterizations. 9Preprint. Under review.

Embedding Bottleneck Following RePlaid, we represent each token by a learned embedding of dimension d = 16 and apply Gaussian diffusion in this embedding space. We normalize each row of the embedding matrix E to unit L2 norm. Before passing a corrupted embedding zℓt to the Transformer, we divide it by p(αℓt )2/d + ( σℓt )2. This keeps its expected squared norm equal to d

across noise levels. We then apply a learned linear projection without bias to map the d-dimensional latent to the Transformer’s hidden dimension. Clean context embeddings are multiplied by √d and passed through the same projection. We use separate optimizer parameter groups for E and the input projections, as specified in Appendix F. 

Self-Conditioning We employ self-conditioning (Chen et al., 2022), where during inference, the denoising model is also conditioned on the output from the previous denoising forward model evaluation. During training, we apply self-conditioning to a deterministically selected quarter of the examples in each global batch. For these examples, we first run the denoiser without self-conditioning under no grad and compute its posterior-mean clean embedding e1: Lθ = E[x1: Lθ ], without re-normalizing its length. We then use this estimate to condition a second forward pass on the same corrupted input, diffusion times, and attention mask, and compute the training loss from that second pass. For these training examples, we stop gradients through the input embeddings (both corrupted token embeddings z1: Lt and clean context embeddings) and the schedule quantities. The second pass still trains the denoiser and its input projections. The self-conditioning input is zero on the clean half of the 2L canvas and on examples not selected for self-conditioning. On batch samples that receive self-conditioning, we supply the estimate only to the noisy half. For conditional tasks, any fixed conditioning tokens present in that half use their known clean embeddings instead of model predictions. Validation uses the same two-pass procedure for every example. At inference, generated positions start with zero self-conditioning and subsequent denoising steps reuse the preceding clean embedding estimate without an additional bootstrap pass. We implement self-conditioning as in RePlaid by multiplying the self-conditioning embedding by √d, applying a separate bias-free linear projection to the Transformer width, and adding it to the projected input. This projection is initialized to zero. 20 Preprint. Under review.

C.2 SLIDING WINDOW TRAINING & E VALUATION 

In Algorithm 3, we present the method for efficiently computing diffusion loss at each position for Sliding Window Clock Diffusion. As in block training, we divide the noisy latent sequence into disjoint groups of S-width token windows. Each window corresponds to a different awake set. However, the Sliding Window Clock Diffusion training algorithm differs from that of Block Clock Diffusion in two ways: 1) the vectorized training attention mask has a different clean-to-clean attention pattern, and 2) diffusion times need to be unique per position and are not shared within an active window. 

Modified Attention Mask Concretely, MSW is identical to MB except that clean-to-clean attention becomes token causal; the noisy-to-clean and noisy-to-noisy quadrants are unchanged, so noisy positions still attend to one another within a width-S window and to clean keys strictly preceding that window. 

Non-Uniform Time per Active Window In block training, every position of a block shares one diffusion time, so a single draw per block suffices. In sliding window generation, each position in the active window of S tokens is at a different time in its denoising trajectory. The schedule in (11) would space those times exactly 1/S apart, but we deliberately train on random spacings instead. We do so to enable Cache Grab samplers (Section 3.6): since these samplers commit a variable number of positions per step, the window advances by a variable stride and adjacent positions can end up separated in time by non-fixed-width intervals. We therefore draw L times t1: L ∼ U [0 , 1] L,one per position, and sort them in ascending order within each of the disjoint length-S groups

## D ADDITIONAL CLOCK DIFFUSION SAMPLING DETAILS 

Denoising Budgets We briefly comment on what the total number of denoising steps looks like under each of the local clock formulations for block and sliding window clocks. For block generation, from (33), we have r = B, so Algorithm 1 runs for T = BN model calls, with one block awake at any time. Every N steps, an entire block completes in lockstep and is added to the KV cache. So on average we decode S tokens every N steps. For sliding window generation, from (11), r = ( L + S − 1) /S , so Algorithm 1 runs for T =(L + S − 1) N/S model calls and commits one position every N/S calls, rather than S positions every N as in the block instantiation. Thus, other than at the end of the sequence, both instantiations commit S tokens every N model calls on average. 

Budgets Below the Window Size: N < S The sliding window clocks in (11) offset adjacent positions by 1/S , while each model call advances every clock by 1/N . When N = S, one position completes per call and Algorithm 1 runs for T = rN = L + S − 1 calls. When N < S , rN is generally not an integer, so we instead group positions into cohorts of S/N consecutive positions 25 Preprint. Under review. that share one clock. One cohort is admitted per model call and committed together N calls later. The window therefore still holds S positions but advances S/N at a time, and sampling takes 

⌈LN/S ⌉ + N − 1 model calls.

Cache Grab-Confidence replaces the commit rule of Algorithm 4 with Algorithm 5. A position is eligible to be committed if its clock has run out, as in standard decoding, or if the probability the model assigns to its arg max token is at least a threshold η. Adapting Cache Grab-Confidence for Block Decoding B-ClockDLMs denoise a whole block in lockstep and commit it at once, so the rule is applied to the block as a unit. After each model call, if every position being generated in the current block reaches the threshold η, the block is committed; otherwise the block takes its next denoising step, and after its N -th call it is committed. The committed block is written to the cache in the next block’s first model call, with full attention within the block (Figure 7, Left ), and the next block starts with samples from the prior. E.2 CACHE GRAB -SSD In Cache Grab-SSD, each iteration makes two model calls. The first is the usual denoising call over the window, from which every active position receives a draft token. The second call runs the model in AR mode over the drafts, attending causally among the draft tokens. At every position in the draft window, the AR head provides its prediction given the cache and draft tokens to its left. The sampler commits the longest prefix of the window on which the draft and the AR prediction 27 Preprint. Under review. agree, together with the AR prediction at the first position where they disagree. Thus every iteration commits between 1 and |A| tokens. A position’s clock running out never commits it: a position that reaches uℓ = 0 stays there and is drafted again until it is committed, as an accepted draft or as the AR correction. This maintains the invariant that the produced sequence matches the one we would see under AR decoding alone. 

Keeping Rejected Positions Self-speculative decoding in discrete diffusion discards the positions it rejects. Cache Grab-SSD instead keeps every position it does not commit, together with its denoising progress. 

Staggering Noise in the New Active Window At the start of each iteration, the window is refilled to S positions. Newly admitted positions start from the prior, at uℓ = 1 . Positions kept from earlier iterations advance by one step per iteration, uℓ ← max( uℓ − 1/N, 0) . To maintain staggered noise in the active window, if the newly admitted positions are not committed via SSD, their local clocks are spread evenly over the gap between the newest position kept from earlier and 1 − 1/N . This ensures that the window stays ordered from clean on the left to noisy on the right. Concretely, if 

ub is the new time of the newest position kept from earlier ( ub = 0 , if no position from earlier remains in the window), the i-th of n surviving new positions, counted from the left, is placed at 

uℓ = ub + in (1 − 1/N − ub); the rightmost therefore lands at 1 − 1/N .

Training For our retrained RePlaid model and ClockDLMs we learn the noise schedule γt and keep endpoints fixed at γ0 = −5 and γ1 = 3 . We use the monotone log-slope schedule model described in Appendix B.4. Full training details are provided in Table 6. 

Evaluation Each configuration generates summaries for all 11 ,490 test articles once with seed 

1 and batch size one per device. We adopt the decoding recipe of Arriola et al. (2026): at most 

180 new tokens are generated, with a minimum of 50 non-[EOS] tokens generated. We apply an exponential-decay length penalty with a factor of 1.1 that starts after 80 tokens, and a repetition penalty swept over {1.0, 1.2}.
