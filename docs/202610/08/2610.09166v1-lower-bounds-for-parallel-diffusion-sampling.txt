Abstract 

Standard diffusion samplers generate samples through repeated evaluations of a learned score function. Parallel sampling methods seek to accelerate generation by trading additional evaluations for fewer sequential rounds. This raises the question of how much sequential dependence is unavoidable, even when many score queries can be made simultaneously. We establish the first polynomial parallel-round lower bounds for diffusion sampling with approximate scores. Specifically, we prove (1) a eΩ( d1/3)-round lower bound for sampling smooth, near-isotropic Gaussian mixtures in Rd, and (2) an Ω( d)-round lower bound for uniform sampling from anisotropic axis-aligned boxes contained in the unit ball. Both bounds hold for arbitrary randomized algorithms making polynomially many queries per round at arbitrary locations and noise levels, with inverse-polynomial score error and constant total variation accuracy. The linear bound is tight for our box family. Our constructions use fixed approximate score oracles that enforce sequential access to hidden information while satisfying the accuracy guarantee at every noise level.

Diffusion samplers generate samples by progressively removing noise through repeated evaluations of a learned denoising model [SDWMG15, HJA20, SE19, SME21], with applications ranging from high-quality image synthesis to video generation [RBL +22, HSG +22, DN21, SCS +22, PX23, MHS +22, HMT +23, BHE23, ZRA23, SPH +23, BRL +23, GYR +24]. The central idea behind diffusion is to learn how to remove noise from data: during training, a neural network learns to denoise examples corrupted by varying amounts of noise. During generation, the learned model progressively transforms random noise into a new sample through a sequence of denoising steps [SDWMG15, HJA20, SE19, SME21]. Beyond generating samples unconditionally, this framework supports generation guided by text descriptions or partially observed images [NDR +22, SCS +22], allowing users to specify desired content, fill in missing image regions [LDR +22, SCC +22], or enhance image resolution [SHC +23, KEES22]. This combination of sample quality and flexible conditioning makes diffusion models useful across a broad range of generative tasks [RBL +22]. A major practical challenge, however, is the cost of generation. Even after training is complete, producing a single sample with a standard diffusion sampler requires repeated evaluations of a neural network, mak-ing sampling computationally expensive. Faster sampling methods seek to reduce these evaluations while preserving sample quality [SME21, LRLZ22, LZB +22, ZBR +23]. Moreover, standard implementations of denoising samplers such as DDPM [HJA20] and DDIM [SME21] evaluate the denoising model sequentially, using each update to determine the input to the next evaluation. Hence, it is an important question in practice to reduce the number of sequential steps. Parallel diffusion sampling aims to shorten the generation of a single sample by evaluating several denois-ing proposals simultaneously. For example, a sampler can guess states at future noise levels, evaluate the model at those states in parallel, and use the answers to refine the proposed trajectory. Methods based on parallel iteration and speculation exploit this possibility to accelerate sampling [SBE +23, TTL +24, CRYR24, HDSA25]. Such methods can trade additional model evaluations for fewer sequential rounds. The relevant resource is therefore how often the sampler must wait for one batch of answers before choosing the next. These developments raise a fundamental question: 

How many sequential rounds of interaction with a learned score model are necessary, even when each round allows polynomially many evaluations? 

To address this question, we must account for how score functions are obtained. In practice, the target distribution is typically known only through training data, so its exact scores at different noise levels are generally unavailable. Diffusion models instead learn score estimates from noise-corrupted samples using a squared-error denoising objective [SE19, HJA20]. This objective naturally motivates accuracy measured on average under the noisy data distribution. Therefore, we assume that learning has produced score estimates with small mean-squared error, and study the sequential rounds needed to sample using these estimates. Approximate scores satisfying distributional error guarantees support efficient sequential sampling. For targets with finite second moment and sufficiently accurate scores, existing results give nearly linear depen-dence on dimension for sampling suitably Gaussian-smoothed targets, with additional assumptions needed to transfer strong-metric guarantees to an unsmoothed target [BDBDD24, CCDR26]. Parallel algorithms can have polylogarithmic dimension dependence under quantitative regularity assumptions on the scores [CRYR24, GCC25, ZS25]. Speculative sampling also gives a eO(√d) expected-round bound for Gaussian-smoothed bounded-support targets with exact denoising oracles [ABC +26]. Its inexact extension requires uniform pointwise accuracy. These results leave open the parallel complexity under the mean-square guar-antee (1) alone. The closest diffusion lower bound, due to [XP26], establishes that eΩ( √d) approximate-score queries can be necessary for constant total variation accuracy. This is a bound on the total number of queries. Their construction hides useful information in a narrow range of noise levels, requiring a search across possible scales. Those candidate scales can be queried simultaneously. A parallel-round lower bound therefore requires an additional obstruction: information learned in one round must be needed to choose informative queries in later rounds. We construct fixed approximate score oracles that enforce this dependence and obtain the first polynomial parallel-round lower bounds in this model. 3Oracle model. # 1.1 Main results 

In this work, we establish the first parallel query-complexity lower bound for diffusion sampling. Both results below concern sampling the target itself to total variation error at most 1/10 . They hold for arbitrary randomized algorithms making polynomially many queries per round, with arbitrary query locations and noise levels, and with inverse-polynomial score error in (1). 

Theorem 1.1 (Near-isotropic targets; consequence of Theorem 3.1) . For all sufficiently large d, there is a family of smooth, near-isotropic Gaussian mixtures on Rd for which sampling requires eΩ( d1/3) adaptive rounds. 

After a fixed public rescaling, these targets have mean of norm o(1) and covariance within o(1) of Id in operator norm. Thus, poor covariance conditioning is unnecessary for polynomial parallel hardness. The targets are equal-weight Gaussian mixtures whose components share a constant-variance isotropic covariance. 

Parallel hardness. Our constructions arrange hidden information in an ordered sequence of blocks. Independent hard instances alone do not impose such an ordering: the exact score of a product distribution separates across blocks, allowing a single query to probe them all. We instead construct a fixed approximate oracle that reveals the hidden contribution of a later block only when the query passes consistency tests involving earlier blocks. A genuine noisy sample contains evidence of the hidden structure in each block and passes these tests with high probability. Controlling both these failure probabilities and the sizes of the suppressed contributions lets the oracle satisfy (1). At noise levels where later blocks still carry hidden information, a query chosen without the earlier hidden information is unlikely to pass the required tests. The main challenge is to preserve this obstruction across every noise level. We coordinate the blocks so that, at each level, either the first unknown block is difficult to pass, or the later blocks are sufficiently smoothed that their scores admit accurate uninformative responses. Consequently, a query cannot bypass an unknown earlier block and obtain hidden information from a later one, except with small probability. This dichotomy applies separately to every query, including queries at different noise levels in the same batch. A batch can reveal the current block, but using that information to choose new queries requires another round. An unexposed block then prevents the output distribution from approximating the target in total variation. The two constructions realize this mechanism with different geometric costs. For near-isotropic targets, we use independent random sets of Gaussian centers, or codebooks, building on [XP26]. Ordering their sizes lets us fit eΘ( d1/3) successive barriers while preserving near-isotropy. For boxes, separated coordinate scales let each barrier occupy a single coordinate, yielding d barriers at the cost of substantial anisotropy. The sequential-exposure principle has antecedents in parallel matroid search [KUW85] and sampling with count-ing oracles [AGR24]. Our construction implements it through score approximations that remain accurate at every noise level. Section 3 and Section 4 give the detailed argument. 4A linear lower bound from anisotropy. While our first result shows that poor covariance conditioning is not necessary for parallel sampling hardness, anisotropy can provide an additional source of difficulty. Here anisotropy refers to widely different variances in different directions, measured by the condition number κ of the covariance matrix. Previous works such as [LV06, JLLV26, CdDPL +24] have demonstrated this hardness and ways to address it in various sampling settings. Our second construction uses a hierarchy of coordinate scales to strengthen the round lower bound to linear in dimension at the cost of anisotropy. 

Theorem 1.2 (Anisotropic boxes; consequence of Theorem 4.1) . For all sufficiently large d, there is a family of full-dimensional, axis-aligned boxes K ⊆ B2(0 , 1) for which sampling from Unif( K) requires Ω( d) adaptive rounds. 

The box family has substantial anisotropy: its coordinate scales decrease geometrically. The candidate intervals are public, but their selected locations are hidden, and no interior point is supplied. This result shows how separated spatial scales can enforce a successive information barrier in every coordinate. For comparison, specializing the analysis of [CCDR26] to our Gaussian-mixture family gives a eO(d5/6)

score-query upper bound under the same oracle guarantee, leaving a polynomial gap (Lemma 3.5). For our box family, a direct recovery algorithm uses O(d) rounds, matching the linear lower bound (Lemma 4.4). This matching upper bound relies on the known candidate-interval geometry. 

Remark. [ABC +26] obtained a eO(√d) expected-round upper bound for parallel sampling Gaussian-smoothed versions of bounded-support distributions using a Gaussian denoising oracle. This oracle returns posterior means, which are related to smoothed scores by Tweedie’s identity. Their main result assumes exact oracle answers, and their extension to approximate answers requires a uniform pointwise bound on the denoiser error over all query locations and noise levels. Our accuracy assumption is distributional: at each noise level, we control the mean-square score error averaged over the true noisy target. This permits larger errors at query locations that have small probability under that distribution. This distinction is essential to our lower-bound mechanism as our fixed approximate oracles exploit this freedom to suppress hidden information at atypical query locations while remaining accurate on genuine noisy samples. Our lower-bound construction relies on the freedom permitted by the distributional guarantee (1), and does not establish a corresponding lower bound under the pointwise accuracy assumptions of [ABC +26]. 

# 1.2 Related work 

Sequential diffusion sampling. Polynomial-complexity guarantees under mean-square score error were established by [CCL +23, LLT23], using early stopping to accommodate nonsmooth or singular targets. Sub-sequent work improved the dimension and accuracy dependence: [BDBDD24] obtained a eO(d/δ 2) bound, [LY25] obtained eO(d/δ ), and [CCDR26] obtained eO(d polylog(1 /δ )) , with intrinsic-dimension refinements. Here δ denotes sampling accuracy, and the bounds require sufficiently accurate scores. For general tar-gets, strong-metric guarantees apply to Gaussian-smoothed distributions. Guarantees for the original target require a weak metric or additional regularity. 

Parallel sampling algorithms. ParaDiGMS [SBE +23] uses Picard iteration to refine multiple denoising steps simultaneously. Theoretical diffusion guarantees [CRYR24, GCC25, ZS25] accommodate mean-square score error together with quantitative regularity assumptions. For example, the round complexity in [GCC25] depends on Lipschitz bounds for both the true and estimated scores. Autospeculative methods [HDSA25] and speculative rejection sampling [ABC +26] provide another approach, with the exact-oracle and pointwise-error assumptions discussed above. Parallel Langevin methods also give favorable dimension dependence for smooth targets under strong log-concavity or log-Sobolev assumptions [SL19, ACV24]. These upper bounds depend on additional parameters beyond the mean-square score error in (1). 

Query and round lower bounds. Lower bounds for exact first-order sampling include sharp one-dimensional results [CGL +22] and condition-number-dependent bounds already for Gaussian targets [CdDPL +24]. 5Other work treats stochastic gradients [CBL22], relative Fisher information accuracy [CGLL23], or non-log-concave targets with value-and-gradient access [HZ25]. For convex bodies, [GRV15] gives a linear sequential membership-query lower bound using boxes, and [Vem26] proves an Ω( d2/(log d)9) expected total-query lower bound for bodies satisfying B2(0 , 1) ⊆ K ⊆ B2(0 , O (d3/2)) . These bounds count queries rather than adaptive rounds. In the counting-oracle model, [AGR24] proves a eΩ( d1/3) round lower bound using se-quential exposure, following the parallel-search framework of [KUW85, KUW88]. Its oracle differs from smoothed-score access. In diffusion, [XP26] supplies the total-query lower bound and codebook ingredients discussed above. Concurrent work [CW26] compares corruption processes, obtaining eO(√d)-query sampling algorithms for random empirical measures on the Boolean hypercube with sufficiently accurate uniform or Gaussian diffusion scores, and a eΩ( d) query lower bound for approximate masked diffusion. Our focus is the sequential dependence that persists when many score queries can be issued simultaneously. 

Organization. Section 2 introduces our notation and oracle model. Section 3 proves the parallel lower bound for near-isotropic Gaussian mixtures. Section 4 establishes the linear lower bound for anisotropic boxes. Parameter choices and detailed proofs appear in Appendix A and Appendix B.

Parallel hardness. Our starting point is the way [KUW85, AGR24] use blocks to limit the information revealed in each round. The common aim is to force one round of queries to reveal information about only one additional block. More specifically, let d denote the ambient dimension. We divide d coordinates into B = poly (d) blocks of dimension m = ⌊d/B ⌋ each. Within each block, we plant an instance that is hard for the query model. Crucially, the sizes of these planted hard instances must be placed on an increasing scale. The reason for this is to account for the power of the oracle: since the oracle can query at different noise levels, we want to make sure that regardless of the noise level σ, only one of the blocks sees this query as informative. # 5 Conclusion and future directions 

We establish polynomial lower bounds on the number of sequential rounds needed for diffusion sampling with approximate scores. For a fixed polynomial query budget per round, we prove a eΩ( d1/3) lower bound for smooth, near-isotropic Gaussian mixtures and an Ω( d) lower bound for anisotropic boxes. The latter matches an O(d)-round algorithm for our box family. Our constructions encode hidden information in an ordered sequence of blocks and use fixed approximate oracles whose informative responses about later blocks require knowledge of earlier ones. The mean-square error guarantee allows the oracles to suppress information on atypical queries while remaining accurate under the smoothed target distribution at every noise level. A progressive simulation argument shows that, with high probability, the answers to each batch of polynomially many queries can be simulated by exposing at most one additional block. We then show that leaving a block unrevealed prevents accurate sampling, yielding a lower bound proportional to the number of blocks. Our results suggest several directions for future work. 1. Tightening the near-isotropic bounds. For our near-isotropic Gaussian mixture family, a poly-nomial gap remains between the eΩ( d1/3) lower bound and the eO(d5/6) upper bound obtained by specializing the analysis of [CCDR26]. Since this upper bound uses a sequential algorithm, exploiting parallel queries may reduce the number of rounds. On the lower bound side, can a sharper analysis or a modified construction improve the exponent? 2. Near-isotropic convex bodies. Our box construction gives a linear lower bound for uniform sam-pling from convex bodies, but its proof relies on strong anisotropy. Can polynomial round lower bounds also be established for uniform sampling from near-isotropic convex bodies using approximate score oracles? This would show that convexity and near-isotropy together do not suffice for efficient parallel sampling in this model. 3. Relating sequential and parallel lower bounds. The eΩ( d1/2) sequential query lower bound of [XP26] raises the question of whether a comparable parallel round lower bound holds for near-isotropic targets. More generally, when can techniques for sequential query lower bounds be adapted to withstand polynomially many queries per round? The counting-oracle setting provides another context for studying this relationship [AGR24, ABC +26]. 4. Other oracle models. Do polynomial parallel lower bounds hold for exact score oracles, or for approximate scores satisfying uniform pointwise error guarantees at every noise level? More broadly, it would be interesting to determine the optimal round complexity of sampling from convex bodies using membership queries and from log-concave distributions using approximate first-order queries. How do these bounds depend on conditioning and oracle accuracy, and when can reductions from optimization to sampling transfer parallel lower bounds while preserving the relevant oracle guarantees and round complexity? 15 Acknowledgments.
