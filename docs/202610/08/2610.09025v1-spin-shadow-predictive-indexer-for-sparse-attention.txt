# Spin: Shadow Predictive Indexer for Sparse Attention 

Yao Fu, Cyrus Chang, Ritchie Zhao, Bryce Long, Yueying Li, Mahdi Kamani, Samkit Jain, Rahul Raman, Tara Safavi, Shreya Gupta, Parsa Ashrafi Fashi, Minseok Lee, Julien Demouth, Bita Darvish Rouhani 

Indexer-based sparse attention reduces the cost of core attention by passing only a fixed, small number of important tokens to it. However, the indexer must still score the entire KV cache at every decoding step. This scoring overhead becomes a major bottleneck as the context length grows. We propose Spin (Shadow 

Predictive In dexer) to reduce this indexer overhead. Spin uses lightweight, history-based prediction to identify important KV blocks, avoiding the need to score the full KV cache at every decoding step. Spin treats KV blocks and speculative decoding as first-class design and implementation considerations. Across extensive evaluations on long-context and agentic benchmarks, Spin achieves 30–40% sparsity while preserving task quality. In end-to-end vLLM serving, Spin improves output throughput by up to 14.9% and reduces median inter-token latency by up to 13.2%. 

# 1. Introduction 

DeepSeek Sparse Attention [11] (DSA) is a sparse attention architecture that relies on an indexer to score each token in the context. DSA and its variants have seen significant adoption among frontier open-source models, including DeepSeek-V4 [12], GLM-5.2 [26], MiniMax-M3 [15], and LongCat-2.0 [17]. Core attention processes a fixed top-𝐾 budget, whereas the indexer scales linearly with context length, making it the dominant bottleneck in long-context inference. DeepSeek-V4’s CSA layer [12] combines the original DSA with a new token-level compressor, effectively reducing DSA’s context length by 4 ×. This, however, does not change the indexer’s linear scaling with context length; thus, at very long context lengths (e.g., 512K–1M), the indexer remains a major contributor to DeepSeek-V4’s decode inter-token latency (ITL). We observe that DSA’s indexer patterns are predictable, similar to temporal patterns previously observed in attention scores [23, 24]. Indexer scores can be positively correlated with (1) previous DSA layers’ indexer scores in the same decode iteration ( prev-layer ) and (2) the same layer’s scores from previous iterations (prev-iter ). Our benchmarking results show that prev-iter signals correlate more strongly with current scores than prev-layer signals. This motivates Shadow Predictive In dexer ( SPIN ), which uses simple per-request statistics of each DSA layer’s scores from previous decoding iterations to predict token importance and skip less important context tokens, thereby sparsifying the indexer input. Skipped blocks receive no score updates and may remain excluded even after becoming important. We address this with random exploration within a fixed block budget to refresh skipped blocks at low overhead. To enable efficient implementation in LLM serving frameworks, Spin applies sparsity at the KV-block level. Spin is training-free and introduces no new auxiliary layers or networks. We implement Spin for the DeepSeek-V4 model class in vLLM [14] for accuracy evaluation. Across the long-context benchmarks LongBench-v2 [4], AA-LCR [3], MRCRv2 [18], and RULER [13], and the agentic benchmark tau2-airline [7, 25], Spin preserves comparable task quality at 30–40% block sparsity. We further integrate Spin with random exploration into vLLM, improving output throughput by up to 14.9% and reducing median inter-token latency by up to 13.2%. Our concrete contributions are as follows: 1. We evaluate various prev-iter and prev-layer prediction signals for the indexer in DeepSeek-V4 and show that prev-iter indexer scores provide the most accurate predictions. 2. We propose Spin to sparsify the indexer based on prev-iter scores and an exploration mechanism and ablate various methods to pool the per-token scores into a block-level prediction. 3. We evaluate Spin on DeepSeek-V4 across long-context and agentic benchmarks and integrate it into vLLM, improving output throughput by up to 14.9% and reducing median inter-token latency by up to 13.2%. 4. We compare exploration methods on MRCRv2 and show that exploration can recover task quality

# 3. SPIN 

3.1. Overview 

Spin extends naive prev-iter score prediction in three ways: 1. Maintain exponential moving averages (EMAs) of previous indexer scores along the vertical and diagonal patterns. This allows Spin to capture longer-term signals in both patterns. 2. Pool the predicted token-level scores into blocks. This allows Spin to skip entire KV blocks and integrate efficiently with paged-attention backends. 3. Reserve a small fraction of the block budget for random exploration. This allows Spin to refresh observations of skipped blocks without increasing the number of blocks scored.

For multi-turn inference, it repeats this reset at the start of each turn. In both cases, the first decode iteration runs without block pruning to initialize the EMA state. We also always retain the sink block and the newest active block. Exploration is described separately below. 

3.2.1. Staleness Mitigation 

With Spin enabled, blocks with lower predicted scores may be excluded from the indexer input and therefore receive no EMA updates. However, these blocks can still become important at later decode steps. Periodic full-indexer refresh revisits all blocks, but increases scoring work relative to the usual sparse steps. We instead reserve part of the existing block budget for exploration. At each iteration, a fraction 𝛾 of the block budget 𝑚 𝑡 is reserved for exploration. The remaining budget retains the forced blocks and the highest-scoring blocks following Algorithm 1. Exploration fills the reserved positions with distinct blocks chosen from those not already selected. Forced blocks count toward 𝑚 𝑡 , and the exploration budget is capped by the remaining capacity. The total number of selected blocks remains unchanged, preserving the block budget. The explored blocks receive fresh indexer scores and EMA updates, allowing previously missed slots to reflect their current importance. We compare several exploration methods and choose random exploration, which recovers task quality with low implementation overhead. Details are presented in Section 4.6.

3.2.2. Speculative Decoding 

For a speculative draft of length 𝑑 , the target model evaluates 𝑑 + 1 verifier queries in a single forward pass, indexed by rows 𝑟 ∈ { 0, . . . , 𝑑 }. Row 𝑟 is valid if the first 𝑟 draft tokens are accepted; row 0 is therefore always valid. These verifier queries share the same context KV cache but have different query positions and hidden states. A potential concern when applying Spin to speculative decoding is that independently predicted block masks for each query may have a large union, reducing the realized block sparsity. However, we observe that the block masks selected for adjacent verifier queries overlap substantially (Section 4.5). Based on this observation, Spin predicts a block mask for each verifier query and uses their union as the shared indexer-input mask. EMA updates are deferred until the verification outcomes are known. If 𝑎 draft tokens are accepted, the observations from rows 0 , . . . , 𝑎 are replayed in order to update the EMA state. The remaining rows are off the committed decode path and are discarded. We evaluate this design using DeepSeek-V4’s native MTP implementation and study its impact on speculative-decoding acceptance in Section 4.5. 

# 4. Experimental Evaluation 

In this section, we evaluate Spin ’s impact on inference quality and performance. We also conduct microbench-marks of its key design choices. Specifically, we answer the following questions: 1. How does Spin affect task quality on long-context and agentic benchmarks? 2. How much does Spin improve end-to-end output throughput and inter-token latency in vLLM? 3. How does Spin interact with speculative decoding, and does applying it during target-model verification affect average acceptance length? 4. How effectively does exploration mitigate staleness, and how sensitive is Spin to the block-pooling method? 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate Spin on DeepSeek-V4 Flash and Pro, using Flash as the primary model. The evaluation covers long-context benchmarks ( LongBench-v2 , AA-LCR , MRCRv2 , and RULER ), the agentic benchmark tau2-airline , and speculative decoding on SPEED-Bench . Unless otherwise specified, sparse experiments use logical blocks of 64 compressed indexer slots, max block pooling, and an EMA decay of 0.5. The LongBench-v2, AA-LCR, RULER, tau2-airline, block-reduction, and MTP results evaluate the core predictor without exploration. The MRCRv2 study compares exploration policies, and the end-to-end implementation uses random exploration with 𝛾 = 0 .05. We use Spin (𝑥 %) to denote a target block sparsity of 𝑥 %, meaning that 𝑥 % is the intended fraction of context blocks to skip. Realized block sparsity is the measured fraction skipped after budget rounding, forced-block retention, and, for speculative decoding, mask union. We compare each Spin configuration with a matched all-keep control, in which no blocks are skipped, using the same model and evaluation protocol. To measure mean and p99 miss, we compute dense indexer scores within the DeepSeek-V4 indexer path and apply Spin ’s block masks before top-𝐾 selection. We report the task-specific score as the primary quality metric and top-𝐾 miss rate (mean and p99) as auxiliary diagnostic metrics. The miss rate is the fraction of ground truth top-𝐾 indices removed due to Spin ’s block-sparse mask. In the MRCRv2 exploration study, the indexer scores only the selected blocks, and we report task scores. Benchmark-specific setup details and hyperparameters are described in their respective subsections. End-to-end serving performance is measured using the integrated vLLM implementation described in Section 4.4.

We ablate two block-reduction design choices in Spin : where in the prediction algorithm slot-level signals are reduced to blocks and how values within each block are pooled. We define three variants, ordered from the latest to the earliest block reduction: • A0: Compute predicted slot scores, select top-𝐾 slots, then expand the selection to blocks. A1 pools predicted slot scores into predicted block scores while retaining slot-level EMA states. A2 performs block reduction earliest, before the EMA predictor. A0 has no pooling operator. # 5. Related Work 

Temporal correlation in attention. It is well documented that attention scores form vertical and diagonal patterns in certain heads; many papers have leveraged these patterns to sparsify and speed up attention during decode. H 2O [27] selects heavy-hitter tokens using accumulated historical attention scores while retaining a separate budget for recent tokens. StreamingLLM [21] instead retains attention-sink tokens together with a sliding window of recent tokens. Several works also analyze the diagonal pattern and its origin in RoPE positional encoding [6, 10, 24]. AttentionPredictor [23] learns to predict next-token attention scores from their temporal history. PRR [20] applies an EMA block predictor to dynamic sparse attention, using predicted blocks to speculatively execute core attention while the original block selector runs and repairing missed blocks afterward. Rather than assuming temporal prediction is the right choice, Section 2 empirically compares previous-iteration prediction against random and cross-layer alternatives and shows that previous-iteration prediction is more accurate. 

Indexer-based sparse attention and its optimizations. Recent work reduces indexer cost through several complementary mechanisms. Cross-layer reuse avoids repeated indexing at adjacent layers. Index-Cache [5], discussed in Section 2, studies training-free and training-aware methods that reuse top-𝐾 indices across DSA layers. GLM-5.2 natively deploys this mechanism from mid-training [26]. HISA [22] reduces indexer cost through hierarchical filtering, running DSA’s original token-level indexer only within blocks selected by a coarse block-level stage. LongCat-2.0 later combines cross-layer reuse and hierarchical filtering with streaming-aware indexing in LongCat Sparse Attention [17]. MiniMax Sparse Attention [15] instead adopts a native block-level Index Branch that selects blocks independently for each GQA group. MISA [28] routes a query-dependent subset of indexer heads to perform full-prefix token-level scoring, while PIVOT [16] amortizes a full-prefix indexer scan across grouped queries. Closest to our work, TISA [2] partitions prefix positions into fixed contiguous groups, refreshes one group per decode step in round-robin order, and reuses cached scores for the remaining groups before running top-𝐾 over the complete score cache. Unlike TISA’s fixed refresh schedule, Spin explicitly models vertical and diagonal temporal patterns with per-slot and relative-offset EMA states and selects indexer-input blocks according to predicted importance. Other work targets later stages of sparse attention. Guess-Verify-Refine [9] uses temporal correlation to accelerate the post-indexer top-𝐾 operator. Spin instead applies temporal prediction before the indexer, using vertical and diagonal slot-level EMAs to prune its input. This introduces partial observations and motivates exploration within the selected-block budget. 

# 6. Limitations 

Spin predicts block importance from historical indexer scores and therefore has limited ability to anticipate patterns not represented in its history, such as sudden attention shifts. In addition, Spin ’s predictions affect their own future observations: once a block is skipped, its indexer scores are not observed, which may create self-reinforcing prediction errors. Random exploration gives skipped blocks another opportunity to be observed, but does not guarantee timely discovery or full task-score recovery. Incorporating other prediction signals is left for future work.

Our current evaluation focuses on DeepSeek-V4 and its native MTP. Although we expect the observations and designs to apply more broadly, this has not been demonstrated on other indexer-based sparse-attention models, such as GLM-5, MiniMax-M3, and LongCat-2.0, or with other speculative-decoding methods. 

# 7. Conclusion 

Spin shows that temporal statistics of indexer scores can serve as an effective proxy for current block importance. Using vertical and diagonal EMA statistics, Spin skips up to 40% of indexer input blocks while preserving comparable task quality across the evaluated long-context and agentic benchmarks. Random exploration mitigates stale observations within the same block budget. The core predictor also retains block sparsity without a visible change in acceptance behavior under DeepSeek-V4’s native MTP. Our integrated vLLM implementation improves output throughput by up to 14.9% and reduces median inter-token latency by up to 13.2%. Spin remains limited by sudden attention shifts and the feedback between its predictions and future observations. Combining temporal history with current-query or cross-layer signals may reduce worst-case misses and is a natural direction for future work.
