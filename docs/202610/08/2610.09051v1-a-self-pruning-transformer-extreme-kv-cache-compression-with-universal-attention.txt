# A Self-Pruning Transformer: Extreme KV-Cache Compression with Universal Attention

Abstract 

The large KV-cache size of modern LLMs creates a barrier to efficient deploy-ment. Recent work has explored replacing attention layers’ RoPE positional embeddings with alternative decay-based mechanisms, which can then be used to prune KV-cache during inference. However, these decay functions have limited expressivity, and in practice devolve into sliding-window-like eviction patterns. In this work, we propose a unifying framework for complementary and novel decay mechanisms, capturing complex key statistics and interactions while preserving expressive RoPE embeddings and Softmax attention. The resulting Universal Attention is a highly ex-pressive and end-to-end trainable architecture, whose composite decay mechanism acts as a natural, adaptive pruning criterion, removing tokens that contribute least to attention computation. Experimentally, Universal Attention achieves state-of-the-art 10 × compression on natural language and synthetic task data, while improving downstream performance com-pared to both state-of-the-art baselines and unpruned oracles. It further demonstrates superior long-context generalization with unprecedented 25 × compression at length 16k. 

1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) power many of the applications we use today (Ope-nAI, 2025; Anthropic, 2026; Google DeepMind, 2026; xAI, 2026). However, they predom-inantly employ softmax-attention layers (Vaswani et al., 2017), which compute pairwise dot-products between all tokens in the input sequence. This entails preserving every token, for each layer, as the “KV-cache” during inference time, consuming gigabytes (if not ter-abytes) of device memory, often several times larger than the model itself. This acts as a significant obstacle to deploying LLMs efficiently at scale. To effectively reduce KV-cache sizes, recent research has proposed the addition of a “forget-gate” to the softmax self-attention formulation, enabling unimportant key/value tokens from the past to be deleted. For example, forgetting transformers (Lin et al., 2025a;b) (FoX) introduces and applies a scalar data-dependent decay term for each new token entering the cache, accumulating over time as an additive bias to the attention matrix. This effectively zeros out certain query-key products after softmax normalization, allowing the corresponding key/value pair to be safely evicted. However, these forget-gates are relatively inexpressive, yielding simple patterns that devolve to soft sliding-windows, which can only evict limited key/value entries before downstream model performance degrades. In this work, we introduce Universal Attention (UA), a unifying framework that combines first- and second-order statistics of stored keys to produce uniquely expressive forget gates. Some components of our framework align with and generalize decay mechanisms from popular Linear Attention models, such as DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2024b; 2025a) & Mamba (Gu

We additionally implement hardware-aware custom UA kernels for high-throughput training. The resulting UA architecture is efficient and expressive, achieving over 10 × KV-cache compression on natural language and synthetic task data, for a 1B transformer model trained on 50B tokens. This memory overhead is lower than state-of-the-art pruning methods and even O(1) linear attention architectures, despite improved downstream performance, even compared to unpruned oracle baselines. Cache pruning in UA is also adaptive and input-dependent, generalizing to sequence lengths not observed during training, with unprecedented 25 × compression in long contexts of 16k. 

2 Related Work 

Shrinking the KV cache is a topical problem with several orthogonal approaches. These include dedicated architectures with sublinear cache growth at inference time, techniques to reduce KV-cache access without reducing the overall size, post-training pruning techniques for vanilla architectures, and combined architectures that use trained forget gates to decay KV cache entries. UA sits at the intersection of these approaches. 

Sub-quadratic architectures: Linear attention models collapse the sequence length into a constant, dstate ×dstate -sized, recurrent hidden state Katharopoulos et al. (2020). However, when input sequence lengths exceed dstate , retrieval from the hidden state becomes noisy. To mitigate this issue, models in this family impose a recency bias by decaying past key/value associations (Gu & Dao, 2023; Dao & Gu, 2024; Yang et al., 2025a). While still highly expressive, these models experience considerable drops in quality for complex long-context tasks compared to Softmax self-attention (Jelassi et al., 2024; Ben-Kish et al., 2025). In addition, despite their O(1) memory in the limit, such models can fail to reduce overhead in practice: for typical model configurations, linear attention consumes greater memory for sequences shorter than 1k tokens - a sizable portion of the usual training length (see Appendix B). Fixes aimed at improving model capacity and performance only exacerbate the issue: hybrid models interleaving full attention with linear attention still have the same break-even point, but become O(L) in the limit, reducing best-case memory savings to 8-10 ×

for typical settings where 1/8 to 1/10 of layers are switched to Softmax (NVIDIA et al., 2025; Lieber et al., 2024; Team et al., 2024). Log-Linear Attention (Guo et al., 2026) maintains multiple state buffers, pushing out the break-even point even further. UA instead applies an expressive decay mechanism to Softmax self-attention directly, avoiding the memory lower-bound imposed by a constant-sized recurrent state. 

Sparse attention: Zaheer et al. (2020); Child et al. (2019); Yuan et al. (2025) compute only a subset of the attention matrix. These techniques range from computing static patterns (such as sliding-windows around the diagonal) to dynamic patterns based on custom data-dependent heuristics. Simple patterns like sliding-window-attention reduce KV-cache sizes but degrade long-context capability unless interleaved with more expressive layers (Jiang et al., 2023; Beltagy et al., 2020; Team et al., 2025), while content-dependent dynamic sparsity provides a more expressive alternative (Zhang et al., 2025a; Shi et al., 2025). These dynamic sparse attention patterns are either implemented by modifying full-attention masks to zero out unnecessary query-key dot-products, or by selectively reading queries and keys from memory to compute a partial attention matrix. Neither implementation reduces KV-cache sizes. 

KV-cache eviction: Methods such as H 2O and SnapKV (Zhang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024) use custom heuristics during the prefill & decode stages of LLM inference to determine which key/value tokens to evict from the KV-cache. More recent methods refine the eviction criterion under the same fixed-budget setting: CAOTE (Goel et al., 2025) combines attention scores with value vectors to minimize the attention-output error induced by eviction, while KeyDiff (Park et al., 2026) avoids attention scores and instead retains geometrically distinctive keys based on key-space similarity. These strategies compress KV-caches to 2Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026 a preset target (e.g., evicting 90% of tokens), while UA’s adaptive pruning dynamically determines which key/value tokens are dispensable. As a result, UA compresses much further at long sequence length, in addition to improving performance more generally. 

Data-dependent forget gates: A range of gate formulations have been added to a variety of models, such as LSTMs (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) and transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017), for a variety of purposes. But from a cache compression perspective, many approaches devolve into soft-sliding-window formulations (Lin et al., 2025a;b; Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997). More expressive mechanisms such as the delta rule (Yang et al., 2024b; 2025a;b) preclude the use of well-established elements of attention, such as SoftMax or RoPE embeddings (Su et al., 2024). UA combines multiple forget gates into an additive bias to the standard attention matrix, allowing us to leverage rich positional encoding via RoPE, targeted retrieval via SoftMax, and more sophisticated decay patterns than sliding-window (see Section 5.3), all at the same time. 

3 Background 

This section introduces the notation and necessary background relevant to UA’s design. 

Softmax multi-head self-attention: Standard softmax multi-head self-attention takes an input sequence of tokens (qi)Li=1, (ki)Li=1, (vi)Li=1 ∈ Rn×d, where L, n & d are the sequence length, number of heads, and head dimension, respectively and computes: Softmax (QK ⊤ + M)V, (1) where M ∈ RL×L is the causal attention mask broadcasted across heads, Q, K, V ∈ Rn×L×d

are tensors formed by stacking qi , ki & vi, respectively. During inference, new tokens need to attend to all previous tokens in K & V, resulting in per-layer KV-caches that consume considerable memory. 

Data dependent forget-gates: To reduce large KV-cache sizes, forget-gates decay past key/value tokens, deleting sufficiently decayed entries from the KV-cache. In particular, FoX (Lin et al., 2025a;b) expresses a forget-gate as an additive bias to the attention matrix. Using terminology from (Lin et al., 2025a), FoX computes a gate: ft = σ(xtw + b) ∈ R for each token in the input sequence and computes: Softmax (QK ⊤ + D)V, (2) where Dij = ∑il=j+1 log ( fl ). These decay-values decrease monotonically from the main-diagonal of the attention matrix, ensuring that cache entries with near-zero gates stay near-zero in perpetuity. Sufficiently decayed keys can thus be safely deleted from the KV-cache. However, because KV-cache entries decay in lockstep, this decay mechanism resembles a sliding-window, an inexpressive KV-cache eviction strategy. UA generalizes and incorporates several such decay mechanisms, to achieve more sophisticated behavior. 

4 Universal Attention 

We propose Universal Attention (UA), a novel framework and architecture that incorporates context-aware decay into softmax attention, inspired by modern linear attention methods and the structure of the attention matrix itself. The result is a model that learns adaptive and dynamic sparsity patterns balancing efficiency with expressivity. Figure 1 depicts a UA layer, incorporating decay into softmax attention as a bias term. However, rather than relying on a single decay mechanism, UA combines multiple context-aware patterns via their geometric mean to form a unified decay rate value for each pair of positions. We also take architectural inspiration from linear attention models, which often apply additional channel-wise sigmoid gates to the intermedi-ate representation (Dao & Gu, 2024; Yang et al., 2025a; Poli et al., 2023). By reducing reliance on sink tokens (Qiu et al., 2025), channel gating allows the decay mechanisms greater freedom to learn expressive patterns. We ablate this component and compare to both gated and vanilla attention baselines in Section 5.1.

Implementation: We codesign UA to be hardware-aligned – interoperating with GQA (Ainslie et al., 2023) and FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022; Dao, 2023; Shah et al., 2024; Zadouri et al., 2026), as current kernels do not support both GQA and float-valued biases. We implement UA as two kernels, separately computing (1) the decay-mask, and (2) the attention-output. Additionally, we apply Activation-Checkpointing (Chen et al., 2016) to mitigate storing the O(L 2) sized decay-mask per-layer in device memory - which would otherwise be re-quired to compute gradients in the backwards pass. Our custom implementation boosts end-to-end training throughput by 1.13 × compared to PyTorch-2. We explain our kernel implementations and end-to-end pipeline in detail in Appendix C. 

5 Evaluation 

Setup: We evaluate Universal Attention in a 1B-parameter transformer following the Llama3 architecture (Grattafiori et al., 2024), which uses RoPE (Su et al., 2024) and Grouped Query Attention (Ainslie et al., 2023). Models are trained for 50B tokens on Dolma v1.7 data (Soldaini et al., 2024), reweighted per Chu et al. (2024). Model and training details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix A. For long-context inputs, we apply YaRN (Peng et al., 2024) as a tuning-free adjustment to RoPE. We report results at two prun-ing thresholds p = ( .05, .15 ), to illustrate performance tradeoffs at conservative/aggressive levels of compression. For UA models, we additionally prune in chunks of 128 tokens during prefill, rather than running the whole input and only pruning afterwards. 

Baselines: We compare against both linear- and softmax-attention baselines: Mamba2 (Dao & Gu, 2024), Mamba2-hybrid (NVIDIA et al., 2025), Gated DeltaNet (GDN) and its hy-brid (Yang et al., 2025a), as well as standard Llama3 and gated-attention Llama3-G. All baselines are trained identically to UA. For context extension, we apply YaRN (Peng et al., 2024) only to the Llama3-based models, where RoPE rescaling is a standard extension recipe. For Mamba2-hybrid, we follow Nemotron-H and keep its native RoPE-free design in the attention layers. For GDN and its hybrid, we retain the base RoPE setup in the main comparison to avoid introducing 6Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026 an additional positional intervention. Appendix I further evaluates GDN-H with YaRN at 8K and 16K, where it improves long-context performance but does not change the overall comparison with UA. To compare KV cache compression efficiency, we also apply H 2O (Zhang et al., 2023), SnapKV (Li et al., 2024), and their pyramid variants (Yang et al., 2024a) to the vanilla Llama3 model. We also include KeyDiff (Park et al., 2026) as an additional baseline using the publicly available kvpress (Devoto et al., 2025) implementation. To match the compression rate of UA, we aggressively set these methods to keep only 10% of the KV-cache. This strong suite of baselines thus encompasses a range of complementary recent approaches to reducing memory overhead, as well as two full-size oracles. Further details are provided in Appendix A. 

Benchmarks: We evaluate the models’ performance on two sets of benchmarks: information retrieval, including LongBench-E (Bai et al., 2024) and RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024), and common-sense reasoning tasks, following Yang et al. (2025a). For each task, we track the size of the stored states and KV-caches (“effective cache size”) using their actual sizes in bytes. 

5.1 Downstream Performance Information retrieval: Table 1 reports results on inputs within the training sequence length, together with effective cache size, for RULER (4K) and LongBench-E (0-4K). On RULER, UA delivers significant improvement, even over unpruned Llama3 oracle models, claiming the highest and second-highest average scores at its two pruning thresholds. This improvement is mostly fueled by gains in tracking and retrieval tasks, though performance on aggregation suffers. Aggregation is a common bottleneck for KV-cache eviction methods, since it requires combining information from broadly distributed context. UA shares this weakness, but unlike SnapKV, H 2O, and KeyDiff, its substantial degradation is concentrated in aggregation. On LongBench-E, the best average is obtained by Mamba2-H, yet UA remains highly competitive under a much tighter cache budget: p=0.15 uses under half the memory of Mamba2-H, and exceeds both Llama3 and Llama3-G scores with only a fraction of their cache size. This favorable comparison to full-cache Llama3 also persists at 3B model scale, with results in Appendix F. Overall, UA offers superior memory-performance tradeoffs. 5.2 Context Length Extension 

To better understand how the memory-performance trade-off evolves with context length, Figure 2 plots average score against effective cache size for RULER (left) and LongBench-E (right). Each curve traces a model across increasing sequence lengths from 1K to 16K, so both cache size and task difficulty vary along the curve. Consequently, a lower score at a larger cache size does not imply that additional cache is harmful, but simply reflects evaluation at a longer and more challenging context. Better configurations at a given context length lie toward the upper-left, and UA consistently traces the strongest frontier as sequence length increases. The two UA variants expose a clear trade-off within the method itself: p=0.05 preserves higher scores, while p=0.15 achieves more aggressive compression. Unlike full-attention baselines such as Llama3 and Llama3-G, whose cache grows linearly with context length and quickly exceeds the plotting range, UA keeps cache usage under 25 MB even at the longest contexts shown. Relative to Llama3, UA with p=0.15 achieves 4.5 ×–45.3 × cache reduction on RULER and 7.9 ×–26.9 × on LongBench-E. The gap widens at longer contexts because UA’s cache plateaus, while Llama3 continues to grow. By contrast, SnapKV and H2O reduce cache at a larger accuracy cost, while hybrid baselines grow the cache with diminishing returns. The full breakdown of benchmark scores by task and sequence length is provided in Appendix I.

We examine UA’s actual learned patterns, uncovering several interesting and spontaneous behaviors. 6 Limitations 

Aggregation tasks: Universal Attention is comparatively weaker on aggregation tasks, which often require combining weak evidence distributed across many positions rather than preserving a sparse set of individually salient tokens. This contrasts with UA’s strengths on retrieval, tracking, and QA, suggesting a trade-off in the types of long-range information em-phasized by different architectures. Our hybrid results in Appendix D further show that this weakness can be mitigated by combining UA with complementary memory mechanisms, making UA suitable as a cache-efficient attention component within broader long-context architectures when stronger aggregation capability is required. 

Additional computation: UA introduces additional computation for constructing its learned decay and pruning masks. Although this enables adaptive, content-dependent retention, reducing the associated overhead remains an important systems optimization target. 

Training from scratch: We evaluate UA as a novel architecture, but given that it generalizes standard softmax attention, a natural question is whether existing models can be adapted to UA, rather than having to pretrain it completely from scratch. Early results in Appendix G suggest that this is possible, but a significant gap remains compared to fully-pretrained UA. Closing this gap would greatly increase the practical feasibility of UA’s deployment. 

Inference optimization: While preliminary inference experiments in Appendix H demon-strate that UA’s reduced cache can translate into lower decode latency, the current imple-mentation does not include a fully fused, inference-optimized dynamic cache manager. Efficiently exploiting dynamically sparse KV caches requires specialized sparse kernels and systems support (Ye et al., 2025), and developing such an implementation remains an important direction for future work. 

7 Conclusion 

We present Universal Attention, a technique that generalizes existing decay mechanisms, introduces a novel decay mechanism, and combines these with RoPE and softmax attention into a single, unified layer. UA’s expressive decay mechanism provides a natural adaptive pruning criterion, yielding superior memory-performance tradeoffs, improving downstream performance with lower memory overhead than even O(1) linear attention models. Our results demonstrate that trainable self-pruning architectures provide a practical avenue to mitigate large memory overhead during inference. 10 Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026

A Training, Model, and Evaluation Details 

Training: All models are trained across 16 NVIDIA A100s in parallel. We employ a learning rate of 3 e − 4, with warmup over 2k steps and cosine decay. Optimizer is AdamW with λ = ( .9, .95 ) and weight decay 0.1. The models presented in Section 5.1, 5.2, and 5.3 are trained on 50B tokens, while the models for ablation in Section 5.4 are trained on 10B tokens. The training data is drawn from Dolma v1.7 (Soldaini et al., 2024) using the data-mixture reweighting adopted by Chu et al. (2024), with a context length of 4096 and 0.5M tokens per minibatch. 

Model: We use the Llama-3 tokenizer (Grattafiori et al., 2024) (vocabulary size 128,256) for all models. Unless noted below, all models have 32 layers with dmodel = 1280. • Base architectures: 

– Universal Attention / Llama-3: nheads =16, nkv =4, dff =4096, θRoPE =5×10 5.Context extension uses YaRN with (βslow , βfast ) = ( 0.7, 35 ).

– Mamba2: dstate =128, dconv =4, dff =3072. 

– Gated DeltaNet: nheads =4, dhead =256, dff =3072. For Mamba2 and Gated DeltaNet, dff is reduced from 4096 to keep total size ≈1B. • Hybrid variants (GDN-H, Mamba2-H): In both hybrids, layers 4, 12, 20, and 28 are replaced with grouped-query attention ( nheads =16, nkv =4). GDN-H retains 

θRoPE =5×10 5; Mamba2-H uses no positional encoding in its attention layers follow-ing NVIDIA et al. (2025). • KV-cache compression baselines: 

– SnapKV: sparsity ratio r=0.1, window size = 32, kernel size = 7, max pooling. 

– H 2O: rheavy =0.05, rrecent =0.05. 

Evaluation: We utilize EleutherAI \lm-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2024) for evalu-ating on LongBench-E (Bai et al., 2024) and the common-sense reasoning benchmarks: WikiText (Merity et al., 2016), LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2019), ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), Social IQa (Sap et al., 2019), and BoolQ (Wang et al., 2019). For RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024), we utilize the official GitHub repository 1, following the default 

synthetic task set.

C Kernel Design 

We detail a memory-efficient Universal Attention layer in listing 1 alongside the triton kernels for both the forward and backwards passes in listings 2 & 3, respectively. 

Decay: The decay mask kernels launch one kernel for both the forward and backwards pass. Thread-blocks tile over rows, computing the decay output (in the forward pass), or gradients (in the backwards pass) for a chunk of columns. 

Memory-efficient attention: The memory-efficient attention kernel launches one kernel in the forward pass and two in the backwards pass. In the forward pass, the attention kernel launches 2D thread-blocks with a block computing one chunk (consisting of multiple rows) of the output tensor. In the backwards pass, we first launch a preprocessing kernel that computes o ⊙ dO similar to FlashAttention. Then we launch the main backwards kernel that computes the gradients of the queries keys and values. The two functions 

mem efficient bwd dkv and mem efficient bwd dq are two subroutines of the main kernel and follow logic similar to FlashAttention-2. However, prior to computing softmax nor-malized values, the decay mask is applied to intermediate attention outputs to correctly compute normalized attention scores. If GQA is applied, our kernel mimics a broadcast on the fly by indexing the correct kv-head of the decay-mask.

D Hybrid Model Results 

We further evaluate UA in hybrid architectures that combine it with complementary sequence-modeling mechanisms. We consider two variants: UA-Softmax , which replaces one out of every eight UA layers with standard full attention, and UA-Mamba , which alternates UA and Mamba layers with an even split. All models follow the same training recipe as our main experiments. To isolate the architectural effects of hybridization, we evaluate these models without KV-cache pruning. Table 5 shows that UA remains effective when incorporated into both hybrid architectures. UA-Softmax achieves performance close to pure UA, with RULER/LongBench-E averages of 48.8/17.6 compared with 48.5/18.1 for UA. UA-Mamba is slightly weaker overall at 47.9/15.9, but reduces the effective cache relative to the full-attention hybrid. The hybrids also exhibit different capability trade-offs. In particular, introducing full-attention or Mamba layers substantially improves aggregation performance relative to pure UA, while reducing tracking performance. This suggests that UA can serve as a cache-efficient, high-resolution attention component within broader hybrid architectures, with full-attention or recurrent layers providing complementary memory behavior when stronger aggregation capability is desired. While this variant performs reasonably at 4K, its performance degrades substantially with 24 Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026 context length, reaching 40.0 at 16K compared with 58.4 for full UA. The key-aware and similarity-aware mechanisms likewise underperform the complete model when used alone, indicating that the three decay signals provide complementary retention behavior. Overall, these results show that UA is robust to alternative query/key normalization choices and that combining multiple decay mechanisms is important for robust long-context performance. H Latency Analysis 

We evaluate the end-to-end inference-time efficiency of UA. An inference-optimized dy-namic cache manager is not implemented in the current system, as efficiently operating on dynamically pruned KV caches requires sparse matrix–vector computation and specialized sparse kernels, which remain active areas of systems research (Liu et al., 2026; Gale et al., 2020). Nevertheless, the current implementation already provides measurable benefits during training and decoding. For prefill, our custom UA kernels improve end-to-end training throughput by approxi-mately 1.13 ×; since prefill exercises the same forward attention path, this suggests that kernel optimization can also reduce TTFT, although we do not report a matched inference-prefill baseline here. For decoding, we evaluate a naive UA inference implementation with and without KV-cache pruning. As shown in Table 9, pruning yields approximately 1.1 ×–1.4 × per-token decode speedup (11–29% lower latency), with larger benefits at longer prompt lengths.
