# Rethinking What to Cache in Few-Step Diffusion Transformers: Solver-Aware Target Selection 

Shuo Yang 1,2,3 , Lihao Fang 1,2 , Yi Zhang 1,2 , Haixiang Wang 1,2 ,Xincheng Ye 1,2 , Shufan Chen 1,2,3 , Jipeng Guo 1, Youqing Wang 1,†

Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) can generate high-quality images and videos, but generating each sample requires multiple costly DiT forward passes. Two common ways to accelerate DiT sampling are step dis-tillation, which reduces the number of sampling steps, and caching, which skips some DiT evaluations by reusing a tensor computed at an earlier step. Most caching methods decide in advance which tensor to reuse. After distillation, adjacent sampling steps are farther apart. Reusing a tensor across this larger gap introduces more error, so choosing what to cache becomes especially important. We therefore introduce 

AutoTarget , a method that chooses the cached tensor for a given model, solver, and reuse schedule. Auto-Target uses a small set of runs without cache reuse to measure the error caused by reusing each candidate tensor, then selects the candidate with the lowest error. We also analyze how an error at one reuse step affects the final sample. For Euler sampling, we identify cache targets that produce the same trajectory and show why a stored solver update may not. Experiments on distilled image and video DiTs show that the best cache target changes with the model, image resolution, and solver. AutoTarget reduces DiT eval-uations and retained cache storage. Generation quality remains close to the corresponding uncached run. On the tested PixArt-LCM and FLUX.1-schnell settings, its calibration ranking matches the ranking from held-out cached runs. To help others reproduce the method, we provide its core implementation on GitHub at https://github.com/wali1024-offical/AutoTarget.

1 Introduction 

Even after step distillation shortens DiT sampling (Salimans & Ho, 2022; Song et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2023), each remaining forward pass in image and video generation is costly (Chen et al., 2024b; Team Wan et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2024). Caching can skip some of those passes (Ma et al., 2024b; Kahat-apitiya et al., 2025), but the larger gaps between the remaining solver steps make a tensor computed at one step less reliable when it is reused at a later step. Many cache methods decide when to reuse a tensor or predict how a stored feature should change, but keep the tensor type fixed. Different types can produce different sampling trajectories. For a given model, solver, and reuse schedule, which tensor should we cache?

Figure 2 shows the distinction between caching a value inside the DiT and caching one of its outputs for the solver. We study the second case. At a reuse step, the stored output is used with a new latent and solver step size. For a linear-flow model using Euler sampling, a DiT prediction can be stored as a velocity (the latent’s rate of change along the sampling path), a clean endpoint (the estimated noise-free latent), or a one-step update (the velocity multiplied by that step’s size). These forms describe the same prediction at the exact step, but need not behave alike when reused later. Velocity and the clean endpoint give the same trajectory if the endpoint is converted at each new step. A stored update, however, retains the earlier step size and can produce a different trajectory when that size changes. This is why the solver must be part of the cache-target comparison. With the reuse steps fixed, AutoTarget chooses what to cache using a few uncached sampling runs. Each run evaluates the DiT at every scheduled step, allowing us to measure the local reuse error of every valid target. Because the candidates represent different quantities, we pass each one through its solver conversion and the frozen VAE decoder before comparing it with the uncached run. If the two lowest errors are too close, AutoTarget makes no selection. Otherwise, deployment uses the target with the lowest calibration error. This selection requires no training and adds no inference-time predictor. All candidates use the same number of DiT evaluations and the same solver sequence for a fair comparison. Figure 4 shows the calibration and deployment stages. Our contributions are as follows. 

Solver-aware cache selection. AutoTarget chooses what to cache for a fixed model, solver, reuse schedule, and storage budget using exact calibration runs. 

Reuse analysis. We show how errors accumulate, when cached forms give the same Euler trajectory, and why stored updates can differ as step sizes change. 

Results across settings. PixArt-LCM selects velocity at 512 px but denoised/clean at 1024 px when only resolution changes (Figure 3). Across image and video DiTs, AutoTarget saves DiT calls and retained storage while keeping quality near uncached runs. 

2 Related Work 

Modern DiT pipelines build on denoising diffusion and score-based modeling (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021b), latent-space generation (Rombach et al., 2022), and transformer denoisers (Peebles & Xie, 2023). These foundations make the repeated transformer forward pass the main unit targeted by both step reduction and cache reuse. 

Few-Step Distillation of Diffusion Models. Step distillation compresses the iterative reverse process of diffusion models into a small number of sampling steps, through progressive distillation (Salimans 

2& Ho, 2022), consistency training (Song et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2023), and distribution-matching or adversarial objectives (Yin et al., 2024b;a; Sauer et al., 2024). These recipes yield the distilled image and video DiTs studied in this work, including PixArt-δ (Chen et al., 2024b;a), FLUX.1-schnell (Black Forest Labs, 2024a), and distilled variants of Wan and HunyuanVideo (Yin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Team Wan et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2024). Distillation increases the gap between adjacent solver coordinates. This makes reuse errors more likely to change the solver trajectory, even though skipping one DiT evaluation still saves substantial computation (Du et al., 2025). AutoTarget works with a frozen distilled model and chooses the tensor to cache. 

Caching and Feature Reuse. Caching methods skip full network evaluations by reusing tensors computed at earlier solver coordinates. Most methods cache predefined internal representations: deep or block features (Ma et al., 2024b; Wimbauer et al., 2024), layer outputs with learned skip routers (Ma et al., 2024a), fast-forwarded blocks (Selvaraju et al., 2024), polynomially reconstructed features (Feng et al., 2025), attention results (Zhao et al., 2025; Lyu et al., 2025), or non-salient token features (Zou et al., 2025); others adapt when to reuse via content-adaptive or timestep-embedding signals (Kahatapitiya et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a). Under the larger inter-step gaps of distilled models, some additionally train predictors that forecast stale cached features (Liu et al., 2025b; Zou et al., 2026). Across these methods, the cached representation itself is fixed by design. 

Cache Target Selection. A few recent methods make the cache policy more flexible. They let the model choose internal features (Bu et al., 2026), assign a reuse solver to each feature dimension (Zheng et al., 2026), profile reusable blocks (Ma et al., 2025), or optimize the reuse schedule over a full trajectory (Chu et al., 2025; Lei et al., 2026). We study a different choice: which tensor passed to the sampling solver should be cached. The candidates include velocity, clean and noise endpoints, and solver displacement. AutoTarget groups candidates with the same solver transitions, then measures local reuse error for each remaining candidate on exact calibration runs. The chosen target depends on the model, resolution, and solver; no predictor is trained. 

3 Method 

Given a model, solver, and fixed DiT evaluation schedule, AutoTarget compares the cache targets supported by the pipeline. It measures their reuse errors on uncached runs and deploys the lowest-error target on new prompts only if its error is sufficiently below the runner-up’s. 3.1 DIT S AMPLING AND CACHE TARGETS 

Let j ∈ { 0, . . . , T } be the discrete sampling-step index, and let t0 > t 1 > · · · > t T be the corre-sponding solver coordinates. Candidate construction. Each pipeline supports a specific set of valid cache targets. We include only quantities that the frozen pipeline already produces, or can convert exactly, at an anchor step. These quantities may include the native model output, velocity, clean and noise endpoints, an LCM denoised residual, and a defined solver update. Every target has an encoder that specifies what is stored and a decoder that specifies how the stored value is used later. We omit a target if its conversion is undefined at any step in the evaluation. We fix the candidate definitions and grouping criteria before calibration. Targets with analytically identical solver transitions form one group. For numerical equivalence, we test the recorded calibra-tion traces using tolerances fixed in advance (Appendix A.1); targets that fail the test remain separate. We then compare one representative per group. All candidates use the same data type, tensor shape, anchor steps, and storage size, as well as the same solver coordinates and coefficients. 3.2 EQUIVALENT CACHE TARGETS UNDER EULER SAMPLING

4 Experiments 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate AutoTarget on few-step image and video DiTs: FLUX.1-schnell, PixArt-LCM, and a distilled 20-step HunyuanVideo model. Within each few-step model comparison, methods use the same weights, solver, sampling coordinates, precision, prompts, and seeds. AutoTarget’s candidate targets share a fixed schedule of full DiT evaluations; baseline methods follow their own reuse rules, so their DiT call counts can differ. 

> 6Figure 4: AutoTarget calibrates cache targets on uncached runs and reuses the accepted target on new prompts.

Evaluation protocol and reporting. We evaluate task quality using ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023), CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), and VBench (Huang et al., 2024), while PSNR, SSIM (Wang et al., 2004), and decoded RGB error measure similarity to a paired reference. Each cached sample and its reference use the same prompt and initial noise, controlling these two sources of variation across paired outputs. For FLUX, the reference is the paired 50-step FLUX.1-dev output (Black Forest Labs, 2024b). For PixArt-LCM, it is the paired four-step output without caching. Held-out paired comparisons report 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. Overall speedup is measured against the full-step reference, whereas cache-only speedup isolates the additional acceleration over the matching uncached few-step sampler. The retained-cache column counts tensors that remain stored between steps, including any required history. Peak device memory also includes temporary workspace and is therefore a separate measurement. Section 4.2.1 analyzes the text-to-image results, Section 4.2.2 presents the results on distilled Hun-yuanVideo, and Section 4.3 studies the effects of cache target selection and calibration size. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS

84.3 ABLATION STUDY 

Figure 9 compares four cache targets on 200 DrawBench prompts with PixArt-LCM at 1024 × 1024 .With the model, solver, and reuse schedule held fixed, denoised/clean achieves the highest PSNR at 23.155 dB; velocity, denoised residual, and ϵ achieve 18.340, 12.844, and 10.007 dB, respectively. To test whether selection depends on the calibration set, we draw subsets of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 prompts from 32 recorded calibration runs and recompute the ranking on 1,000 sampled subsets per size. Every trial selects denoised/clean, which also has the highest PSNR in the 200-prompt compari-son. Thus, a small calibration set consistently identifies the same target in this configuration, even as the prompt subset changes. For further validation, Appendix D.1 reports held-out rankings and calibration errors, together with reduced-step controls, baseline threshold sweeps, and a comparison of two solvers. 4.4 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

AutoTarget selects a cache target for a fixed schedule of full DiT evaluations. Changing only PixArt’s image resolution reverses the ranking of velocity and denoised/clean, while the solver comparison changes which candidates are valid and the order of the lower-ranked targets. These results show that the preferred target depends on the sampling configuration, even when the candidate tensors are derived from the same model prediction. Decoded calibration error provides an empirical ranking whose reliability must be tested on held-out prompts. The solver-state bound explains error propagation, but does not establish that this ranking transfers to other prompt distributions. We therefore fix the selection rule before held-out evaluation and compare complete cached runs, where errors accumulate across reuse steps. Recalibration is required when the model, solver, schedule, or precision changes, and each candidate must have a valid conversion to the solver input. Once calibration is complete, deployment retains only the accepted tensor and requires no candidate comparisons, external quality model, or learned predictor. The shifted LTX-Video setting (HaCohen et al., 2024) illustrates why the acceptance rule is needed. Here, γ = 1 .004 , and the calibration winner outperforms the alternative on only 55% of held-out prompts. Always deploying this target incurs 7.36% regret relative to the best target for each prompt. Because the risk ratio falls below the fixed 1.10 threshold, AutoTarget abstains, allowing the appli-cation to retain uncached sampling or a previously validated cache policy when calibration does not clearly separate the candidates. 

5 Conclusion 

We presented AutoTarget, a method for selecting which tensor to cache in few-step diffusion trans-formers. Given a fixed reuse schedule, it compares candidate targets using uncached sampling runs and deploys the lowest-risk target when calibration clearly separates it from the runner-up. This pro-cedure requires no learned predictor and accounts for how the stored tensor is converted and used by the solver. Our analysis identifies equivalent targets under Euler sampling and relates local reuse errors to their accumulation along the sampling trajectory. Experiments on distilled image and video models show that the preferred target changes with the sampling configuration. Target selection provides additional acceleration over uncached few-step sampling with a small cache. Paired evaluations distinguish reference fidelity from task quality, while held-out tests assess calibration rankings during complete cached runs. When candidates are insuffi-ciently separated, AutoTarget declines to select a target, leaving the fallback policy to the application. These results support treating the cached tensor as an explicit design choice alongside the reuse sched-ule, guided by calibration for the intended model and solver.

A AutoTarget Procedure 

AutoTarget has two stages: calibration and deployment. Both stages use the same model, solver, coordinate sequence, and steps for full DiT evaluation. During calibration, all candidates are tested from the same exact states. Deployment keeps only the selected target. A.1 CANDIDATE CONSTRUCTION 

At every anchor step, the pipeline provides the model prediction used by the solver. AutoTarget con-verts this prediction into the cache targets supported by the current parameterization. In the controlled Euler example, the candidates are velocity, the clean endpoint xclean , and the noise endpoint ε. An-other pipeline may also provide a solver update or another tensor with the same shape as the model 

> 12

output. When candidates are compared under an equal storage budget, they use the same data type and tensor shape. For each calibration prompt, an exact run records the latent, model prediction, coordinate, and solver state at every step. At a reuse step, every candidate is decoded from the same anchor and receives the same exact current state. The original solver then converts the candidate into the next latent, or into the intermediate latent expected by the pipeline. This procedure compares all candidates at the same point in the computation. For PixArt-LCM, the boundary-conditioned denoised prediction and predicted clean endpoint are grouped only if their BF16 one-reuse terminal latents have relative L2 difference at most 10 −6 and their decoded RGB mean squared error is at most 10 −10 on every calibration trace. These tolerances were fixed before calibration. Otherwise, both remain separate candidates. The denoised prediction represents the group when the checks pass. A.2 SELECTION AND DEPLOYMENT 

We apply the same pipeline conversion and frozen VAE decoder to the candidate and exact reference. AutoTarget averages their decoded RGB error over prompts and reuse steps, then selects the target with the lowest average error. If a group of targets is analytically identical or passes the numerical check above, one representative is enough for calibration. During deployment, the steps assigned to full DiT evaluation compute a new prediction and refresh the selected tensor. At a reuse step, the selected decoder reads that tensor and the original solver performs its usual transition. Deployment contains no branches over candidates, external quality model, or learned predictor. For a calibration set P and T sampling steps, the exact runs require |P| T DiT evaluations. Once these exact states are available, testing more candidates requires only conversions and VAE decoding. Those operations add to the one-time calibration cost but do not require more DiT evaluations. In the measured PixArt-LCM calibration, four exact runs take 1.314 seconds, including text pro-cessing. Decoding their exact VAE latents takes 1.528 seconds. Candidate conversion takes 0.059 seconds, and candidate VAE decoding takes 7.533 seconds. The total is 10.457 seconds, excluding the separately measured model load of 7.988 seconds. Each later generation saves 0.0688 seconds in this setting, so about 152 generations recover the one-time calibration cost.

E Scope and Limitations 

The general analysis applies to a discrete sampling solver when its transition on the complete state satisfies the local stability condition in Eq. (27). For Euler sampling with the linear-flow parameter-ization, we give a closed-form proof of which targets are equivalent. For other solvers, AutoTarget can construct and compare candidates in the same way, but their ranking must be measured from that solver’s transitions. The calibration error applies to the tested model, solver, coordinate sequence, and reuse schedule. It does not guarantee the same ranking after any of these choices changes or on every prompt distribu-tion. The decoded VAE error is the score used to compare targets in practice. The theoretical bound measures error in a state that also contains the cache and solver history. Our empirical and theoretical claims concern these two different quantities. Cache reuse and step removal are different operations. In our control, both methods run the DiT at the same anchor steps. They use the same number of DiT evaluations. Cache reuse still executes every transition in the original four-step solver sequence. The reduced-step run executes the solver only at the anchors. Their fidelity is nearly equal for the tested anchors. By comparison, the standard three-step PixArt-LCM schedule is much less faithful to the four-step reference. These results apply only to the tested schedules and solvers.
