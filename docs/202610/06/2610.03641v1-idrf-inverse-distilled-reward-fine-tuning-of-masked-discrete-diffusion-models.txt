## ABSTRACT 

Masked discrete diffusion models offer a promising alternative to autoregressive generation, but iterative sampling can be costly, and intractable sequence like-lihoods complicate reward fine-tuning. We introduce IDRF , a framework for reward fine-tuning of few-step masked discrete diffusion generators. Starting from a standard reverse-KL-regularized objective, IDRF replaces the intractable sequence-level KL penalty with inverse-distillation regularization. With an optimal auxiliary denoiser, we prove that the population inverse-distillation loss upper-bounds the sequence-level KL divergence to the reference distribution. IDRF optimizes a trajectory-based surrogate of this loss without reference-model roll-outs, so the student keeps its own few-step sampler. We view few-step generation as a finite-horizon Markov decision process and optimize reward with a clipped policy-gradient objective over the student’s trajectories. Across DNA, image, and text generation, IDRF achieves high reward with up to 32 × fewer denoising steps than the reference while mitigating reward hacking and preserving sample quality.

Reward-only fine-tuning hacks the reward: DNA lacks accessibility (ATAC-Acc), images blur, and reviews degenerate into repetition that rarely ends with EOS. IDRF avoids these failures. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Masked diffusion models (MDMs) generate sequences by unmasking tokens in parallel, with applica-tions to language, biological sequences, and discrete image representations (Sahoo et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a). Reward fine-tuning adapts these models to task objectives, such as DNA activity, image attributes, or review sentiment. Optimizing reward alone, however, can destroy properties the reward does not measure (Figure 1). 1arXiv:2610.03641v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 KL regularization toward a pretrained reference provides a standard safeguard (Ziegler et al., 2019; Korbak et al., 2022), but an MDM’s sequence likelihood marginalizes over all denoising trajectories and is intractable. Existing reward fine-tuning methods use likelihood surrogates and evidence bounds (Zhao et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a), or penalize KL between reverse transitions (Wang et al., 2025a). The latter requires reference evaluations along the rollout and ties the regularizer to its sampling path. We seek a tractable sequence-level KL regularizer that lets the student use its own sampler. We introduce Inverse-Distilled Reward Fine-tuning (IDRF) , which combines trajectory-based policy gradients with inverse distillation (Li et al., 2026). The regularizer compares the denoising losses of a frozen reference and a fitted auxiliary on student samples. It requires neither the reference’s likelihood nor its rollouts. Both the reward and the regularizer reuse the student’s own trajectories (Figure 2), so the regularizer shapes the student’s few-step sampler directly. Reward fine-tuning requires neither pretraining data nor reward gradients. Our contributions are as follows. 1. With an optimal auxiliary denoiser, we establish a sequence-level KL bound for the population inverse-distillation objective in masked diffusion, valid for any sequence length and any student sampler ( M 3.1). 2. We regularize reward fine-tuning with a practical inverse-distillation objective on the student’s own trajectories, without reference rollouts. The resulting framework supports reward fine-tuning directly in the few-step regime ( M 3.2). 3. Across DNA, images, and text, IDRF reaches high reward with up to 32 × fewer denoising steps than the reference while preserving properties lost under reward-only fine-tuning ( M 5).

IDLM uses inverse distillation to train a fast discrete generator from a pretrained denoiser (Li et al., 2026). Pretraining fits a denoiser to a given data distribution. Inverse distillation reverses this direction by fixing the reference denoiser fref and learning a student distribution pθ on which its predictions remain optimal. In the ideal setting, a student matching p∗ would produce samples that the reference can denoise as well as any model fitted specifically to those samples. A low reference loss alone is not enough to ensure this match. For example, a student that repeats a single sequence may be easy to denoise despite covering very little of the data distribution. Inverse distillation accounts for how predictable the student’s samples are by comparing the reference with the best denoiser for those same samples. This gives the objective 

LID (θ) = LNELBO (fref , p θ ) − min

LNELBO (f, p θ ). (4) Minimizing this gap encourages the student to produce samples on which refitting the denoiser offers little improvement over the reference. This objective also has a distribution-matching guarantee. With a Bayes-optimal reference for p∗ and an exact inner minimum over a sufficiently expressive denoiser family, IDLM shows (Li et al., 2026, Theorem 3.1) 

LID (θ) ≥ DKL (pθ ∥ p∗) ≥ 0, (5) so a zero gap means that the student matches the data. IDLM proves this bound for a single token and for a joint reverse process over whole sequences, and leaves the sequence objective used in practice, a sum of per-token denoising losses, to future work (Li et al., 2026, App. D.2). Proposition 1 proves the bound for its population loss and an arbitrary reference. In practice, a trainable auxiliary denoiser fψ approximates the inner minimum by learning to recon-struct masked student samples. Training alternates between fitting the auxiliary and updating the student to reduce the difference between reference and auxiliary losses. The reference remains fixed throughout. IDRF uses this denoising gap as the regularizer for reward fine-tuning.

## 4 RELATED WORK 

Distillation of masked diffusion models. Several methods reduce the sampling steps of a pretrained masked diffusion model. SDTT distills multiple teacher steps into fewer student steps (Deschenaux & Gulcehre, 2025), and Duo adapts consistency distillation to uniform-state discrete diffusion (Sahoo et al., 2025). Distribution-matching methods instead train a few-step generator to match the teacher’s distribution, through an integral KL objective in DiDi-Instruct (Zheng et al., 2026) or inverse distillation in IDLM (Li et al., 2026). These methods reproduce the teacher, whereas IDRF (ours) uses inverse distillation to stay close to it while optimizing a reward. 

Reward fine-tuning of masked diffusion models. To handle intractable sequence likelihoods, d1 and wd1 use denoising likelihood approximations (Zhao et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026), while SPG uses lower and upper evidence bounds (Wang et al., 2026a). Beyond reward fine-tuning, TUBE provides a further upper bound on the evidence of masked diffusion models (Ivanov et al., 2026). Alternatively, AGRPO treats denoising as an MDP with tractable stepwise action probabilities (Zhan, 2026), and TraceRL updates policies along recorded inference trajectories (Wang et al., 2026b). DMPO and Discrete Tilt Matching learn reward-tilted distributions (Zhu et al., 2026c; Chen et al., 2026). DRAKES instead uses differentiable relaxations for biological sequence design when reward gradients are available (Wang et al., 2025a). 

Joint distillation and fine-tuning. In continuous diffusion, Diff-Instruct++ regularizes one-step reward optimization with an integral KL (Luo, 2025), DMDR combines distribution matching with reinforcement learning (Jiang et al., 2025), and RTDMD uses reward-tilted distribution matching for few-step flow generators (Huang et al., 2026). For masked image diffusion, Soft-Di[M]O refines a one-step generator through soft embeddings. Its adversarial refinement uses real images, and its reward fine-tuning requires differentiable rewards (Zhu et al., 2026b). IDRF (ours) uses inverse distillation as the regularizer of trajectory-based policy gradients, supports reward fine-tuning directly in the few-step regime, and requires neither pretraining data nor reward gradients.

## 6 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

We introduced IDRF, a framework for reward fine-tuning of few-step masked discrete diffusion models using inverse-distillation regularization. Across DNA, image, and text generation, IDRF achieves high reward with fewer sampling steps while mitigating reward hacking. Future work includes scaling IDRF to larger models and extending it to additional domains. 

Limitations. In practice, IDRF involves an adversarial min–max game between the student and the auxiliary denoiser. As in other adversarial training methods, this can introduce optimization instability and sensitivity to the balance between their updates. The auxiliary also adds training cost. Our practical surrogate admits a related KL guarantee for a mixture of one-step completion distributions, while controlling the final sampling distribution remains an open question. 9AI USE STATEMENT

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work develops a method for reward fine-tuning of masked diffusion models and involves no human subjects. All experiments use public datasets and pretrained models: the enhancer activity data of Gosai et al. In particular, steering text toward a target sentiment could be misused to produce deceptive reviews at scale, and our sentiment experiment serves only as a benchmark for reward hacking.

## B SAMPLING AND POLICY -GRADIENT DETAILS 

The ancestral derivation uses a fixed schedule and a final transition to t0 = 0 . If a numerical implementation stops at a positive noise floor, any remaining masks are filled by a final categorical draw. Its sampled choices and log-probabilities must also be included in the recorded trajectory and reward loss, and this extra evaluation counts toward the sampling cost. For max-confidence sampling, masked positions are ranked by their maximum predicted probability and revealed according to a fixed step budget. Recomputing their probabilities with the recorded reveal sets held fixed gives the surrogate described in Section 3.2. We use linear ancestral time grids ending at 10 −5 for DNA and 10 −3 for images and text. For MaskGIT, the reward loss uses four transitions sampled uniformly without replacement from the full 15 recorded trajectory, including cleanup when present, with the sum scaled by the trajectory length divided by four. GM adapts the centered estimator of Hoogeboom et al. (2026, Eq. 14) to variable-length text. For a candidate set and a reference set of 1,000 reviews each, we compute the mean gradient of GPT-2-large’s token NLL on two disjoint subsets of 50 reviews each from each set and take the inner product of the two candidate-minus-reference differences. The reported value averages 10 such estimates, with one standard error across them. Each review contributes its mean token NLL, conditioned on BOS and including one EOS. Gradients are computed in float32 and inner products in float64. The estimator is unbiased for the squared distance between mean gradients, so two samples of the same distribution score zero in expectation. Table 10 uses two references from the held-out validation split. One holds 1,000 of the 2,748 eligible real one-star reviews, and the other 1,000 of the 21 ,448 real reviews of every rating. Each reference is compared against a disjoint sample of the same distribution, and both real-versus-real estimates (0.03 and 0.06 ) are consistent with zero.
