Abstract 

Autoregressive generation in Large Language Models (LLMs) is constrained by the memory and computational demands of attention mechanisms. Sparse attention methods mitigate this cost by selecting only high-probability entries of the attention matrix. We observe that in many such methods, this renders the probability-value multiplication negligible, shifting the bottleneck to the query-key step. Key heads can therefore be reduced to accelerate inference, while retaining more value heads preserves capacity with limited additional decoding cost. We introduce Sparse Asymmetric Group-Query Attention (SAGA), which decouples key and value head counts to exploit this principle, and pair it with approximate top-N (Atop-N) attention, a simple sparse attention method designed to study the interaction between sparsity and head-count asymmetry. We formalize the benefits of this asymmetry theoretically and validate them empirically through latency measurements and quality evaluations on models up to 1.5B parameters. Together, SAGA and Atop-N achieve end-to-end decoding speedups exceeding 2× over our full-attention GQA baseline at long contexts. Models trained from scratch with SAGA nearly match the quality of comparable GQA variants on the evaluated benchmarks. To facilitate adoption, we introduce an efficient fine-tuning method that converts pretrained models to the SAGA architecture, enabling practitioners to benefit from our approach without costly retraining. 1

1 Introduction 

Attention-based architectures have become dominant across AI applications [1], with large language models such as GPT [2, 3], Llama [4], and Qwen [5, 6] demonstrating strong capabilities in natural language understanding [7], generation [8], and reasoning [9]. However, the quadratic scaling of attention with sequence length [10, 11] remains a significant bottleneck, constraining context window sizes and limiting the ability of models to reason over lengthy documents. For autoregressive generation, KV caching [12] provides substantial speedups by reusing key and value computations across generation steps. This shifts the bottleneck from compute capacity to

memory capacity and bandwidth [13]. Group-Query Attention (GQA) [14] further exploits this setting by reducing the number of key-value heads, accelerating inference for long sequences. Yet even with these optimizations, bounded context windows remain a practical necessity. Sparse attention methods [15–19] offer a complementary direction, accelerating attention by com-puting only a subset of query-key interactions. Such methods span both the prefill and decoding phases, and range from zero-shot approximations to approaches integrated into pre-training. In this work, we focus on the decoding phase and use a generic sparsity method, as primary examples of the effects of sparsity on the computational profile of generation. To this end, we adopt top-N attention as a representative sparsity method. Building on previous observations [20, 21] that models assign significant attention weight to only a small subset of keys, top-N attention has the advantage of sim-plicity: it requires no training to apply and admits a portable implementation, a property uncommon among sparse attention methods. Our efficient approximate top-N attention (Atop-N) implementation gathers only the most relevant attention entries, reducing the probability-value multiplication to a fraction of its original cost. Analyzing the resulting computational profile reveals a fundamental asymmetry: under sparse atten-tion, the bottleneck shifts from the probability-value multiplication to the query-key multiplication. This insight motivates Sparse Asymmetric Group-Query Attention (SAGA), an architectural modifica-tion that decouples the number of key heads hk from value heads hv . While GQA ties these together, SAGA reduces hk independently, directly accelerating the query-key multiplication. The number of value heads remains large to preserve model capacity, incurring no additional cost since only the top-N values are accessed. We formalize this asymmetry theoretically and validate it empirically, demonstrating end-to-end speedups exceeding 2× in long contexts while maintaining the majority of baseline quality. To facilitate practical adoption, we introduce an efficient fine-tuning method for converting existing GQA models to the SAGA architecture. We merge teacher key heads using a closed-form initialization derived from minimum-weight perfect matching on attention logit reconstruction error (Sec. 4), followed by a short distillation phase. This enables the benefits of SAGA without costly retraining. To summarize, our contributions are: • We present a novel analysis of LLM inference trade-offs with accelerated sparse attention. • We introduce Atop-N, a simple and portable sparse attention algorithm for decoding. • Motivated by our finding, we propose SAGA, an extension of the GQA architecture tailored for sparse attention inference. We show that SAGA is competitive both when trained from scratch and when finetuned from existing LLM transformers, while demonstrating more than 2× runtime speedups end-to-end on long sequence generation. 

2 Background and related work 

2.1 Multi-head self-attention 

The Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al . [1], revolutionized natural language processing. Its core mechanism, multi-head self-attention, enables parallel processing of sequences and captures long-range dependencies by allowing the model to attend to all positions simultaneously. Key-Value (KV) caching [12] is an optimization technique that significantly improves the inference efficiency of autoregressive Transformer models. During the generation of each new token, Trans-former models would typically recompute the key and value projections for all previous tokens in the sequence. The KV cache stores projections from previous steps, eliminating redundant computations. For an autoregressive model generating the lkv -th token, the attention computation with KV caching can be expressed as:

By caching key and value vectors from previous time steps, the model only needs to compute new projections for the most recently generated token, leading to substantial speedups during inference. This optimization is particularly valuable for long-form text generation, where the model must generate many tokens sequentially. The trade-off is increased memory usage proportional to sequence length, as the cache must store key-value pairs for all tokens in the context window. 

2.3 Group-query attention and head reduction 

Group-Query Attention (GQA) [14] addresses the memory overhead of KV caching by recognizing an asymmetry in the attention computation during decoding: there are significantly more cached keys and values than queries ( lkv ≫ lq ). In this context, it is sensible to allocate more of the model’s capacity to the queries, which are computed fresh at each decoding step, rather than to the keys and values, which are retrieved from the cache. GQA achieves this by decoupling the number of key-value heads hkv from the number of query heads hq , such that hq > h kv . Multiple query heads share a single key-value head, reducing the size of the KV cache while preserving the expressiveness of the query projections. This approach yields a significant improvement in inference efficiency with negligible degradation in model quality, making it a widely adopted technique in modern LLM architectures. Beyond reducing the total number of KV heads, recent work has explored making key and value head counts asymmetric. DHA [22] fuses similar attention heads in MHA architecture into decoupled per-layer key and value configurations to reduce memory footprint. Sigma [23] performs differential rescaling of query, key, and value dimensions during pretraining and finds that reducing key capacity is preferable to reducing value capacity—an observation consistent with our analysis. While concep-tually related, SAGA differs in both motivation and applicability, exploiting the shifted computational bottleneck under sparse attention to accelerate decoding. Multi-head latent attention with DeepSeek Sparse Attention [24, 25] is related in spirit but must be trained from scratch with a shared latent KV and a learned indexer; it reduces the number of key positions scored per query, whereas SAGA reduces the number of key heads , so the two are orthogonal rather than competing. 

2.4 Attention sparsity and KV cache acceleration 

Recent efforts to mitigate the quadratic complexity of the attention mechanism have largely focused on attention sparsity and KV cache compression. Sparse attention methods, such as BigBird [17], Longformer [16], and Sparse Transformer [15], utilize predefined patterns like sliding windows or global tokens to restrict the number of query-key pairs computed. While effective for the prefill phase, these static patterns often struggle to capture the dynamic, data-dependent importance of tokens during autoregressive decoding. NSA [19], SpargeAttention [26], BLASST [27], and BSFA [28] also aim to bridge the gap between algorithmic sparsity and hardware efficiency, mostly focusing on the more compute-intensive pre-fill phase. Our work, SAGA, focuses instead on the decode phase, and offers a new framework by combining a simple attention sparsity method with a dedicated neural architecture. Other methods accelerate decoding by evicting low-importance tokens from the KV cache [29, 30] or selectively loading cache pages using query-aware metadata [31, 32]. 2.5 Top-N attention 

Top-N attention [20, 35] (also known as top-K attention or kNN attention) is based on the observation that only a small subset of attention keys meaningfully influence the attention output. This sparsity arises from the softmax operation, which effectively learns to identify the best query-key matches while suppressing mismatched pairs. Formally, top-N attention identifies the indices of the N largest values in each row of A:

ij,k, 1: N = TopN (Aj,k, 1: lkv ), (6) and masks all other entries of A not in i before applying the softmax. This formulation has been shown to enable memory-efficient implementations [20, 35], though it does not yield computational speedups since the full attention matrix must still be computed. Several recent works have extended top-N attention in various directions [36, 37] or strengthened its theoretical foundations [38]. 

3 Method 

We begin by introducing the attention sparsity method underlying SAGA. For clarity, we present an architecture-agnostic formulation of top-N attention to illustrate our approach and the trade-offs many online sparse attention method induce. In the following analysis, we show that under sparse attention, the computational bottleneck shifts from the probability-value multiplication to the query-key multiplication—the property that SAGA is designed to exploit. 43.1 Efficient approximate top-N attention for LLM decoding 

We propose an efficient framework for top-N attention designed for LLM decoding. Using the indices i ∈ Zh×lq ×N from Eq. (6), we define the Top-N Attention Matrix A[i] ∈ Rh×lq ×N and corresponding Top-N Values V[i] ∈ Rh×lq ×N ×d, and compute attention over only the gathered entries: 

O = softmax( A[i]) V[i]. (7) In autoregressive generation ( lq = 1 ), this reduces the probability-value cost from O(h l kv d) to 

O(h N d ), potentially saving nearly 50% of computation and bandwidth when N ≪ lkv . However, naïvely finding the true top-N entries incurs lengthy hardware runtimes, negating the benefit. We address this with an approximate threshold approach: given the N -th largest attention value as a threshold, top-N entries can be identified by a simple per-element comparison — a parallel, portable operation much faster than sorting. Since attention distributions shift only marginally between consecutive decoding steps, we compute exact thresholds once every n tokens and reuse them, amortizing the cost over the full text generation. We term this method Atop-N attention; full implementation details are in Sec. C. For long sequences, Atop-N yields an overall speedup of nearly 2× for the full attention operation. Section 5 shows that for practical values of N , Atop-N retains 83–97% of baseline RULER accuracy. 

3.2 Sparse Asymmetric Group-Query Attention 

The analysis in the previous section reveals that under Atop-N attention, the probability-value multiplication accesses only hq × lq × N value vectors, N per query head, regardless of the number of key-value heads hkv . Consequently, hkv affects computational complexity only through the query-key multiplication in Eq. (2). This asymmetry suggests that the number of key heads and value heads should be disentangled. We propose Sparse Asymmetric Group-Query Attention (SAGA), which decouples the key and value head counts into separate hyperparameters hk and hv . Reducing hk directly reduces the memory bandwidth required to load keys during the query-key multiplication step (Eq. (2)), the dominant latency bottleneck in LLM decoding under Atop-N attention. The number of value heads hv can remain large to preserve model capacity, since only the top-N values are accessed regardless of hv . A diagram of SAGA is shown in Fig. 1. Beyond the bandwidth reduction, fewer key heads yield a smaller key projection WK (reducing per-token compute) and a smaller KV cache footprint, enabling longer context lengths or larger batch sizes within the same memory budget. SAGA can be applied directly when training from scratch, as detailed in Sec. 5.2. To leverage existing pretrained models, we describe a procedure for converting GQA models to SAGA in Sec. 4 below.

5.3 Application to pretrained models 

To evaluate SAGA on models with realistic capabilities, we convert pretrained models to the SAGA architecture via knowledge distillation, avoiding the compute and data requirements of training a competitive LLM from scratch. We start from Qwen2.5-1.5B [6], a highly capable model with only 

hk = 2 key heads, where the matching from Sec. 4 is trivial (a single pair) and reduces to averaging (hk : 2 → 1). We then follow up with Llama 3.2-1B [39], using the full matching-based initialization from Sec. 4 to merge 8 key heads into 4 (see Sec. B.1 for a comparison of merging strategies). Ideally, a fair comparison would increase the student’s capacity to compensate for the reduced key heads, for example by increasing the head dimension or adding value heads. However, as teacher and student representations diverge, a short distillation cannot effectively leverage the teacher’s signal. For simplicity we keep the student architecture identical to the teacher except for the reduced hk, and accept the slight quality degradation from this conservative approach. The student is initialized by merging the teacher’s key projection heads and trained with a combination of KL divergence from the teacher and hard-label cross-entropy. We train in several stages to expose the student to increasing context lengths while keeping total training short at only around 23K iterations (architecture dependent). We use a mix of SlimPajama [43], QA [44, 45] and long-context data [46, 47], as well as synthetic needle-in-a-haystack examples. We note that the student is heavily weighted to match the teacher’s output probabilities on these sequences, so its retained performance reflects architectural capacity rather than task-specific adaptation. Full hyperparameters and data mix details are provided in Sec. C.3. The distilled student retains 96.3% of the teacher’s aggregate RULER score across 8K, 16K, and 32K context lengths, with per-task results shown in Fig. 4. A complementary Llama 3.2-1B comparison is shown in Sec. D. As shown in Tab. 1, the SAGA student remains close to the teacher on general-capability benchmarks despite the reduced key head capacity. For comparison, we evaluate alternative decode-acceleration methods: sliding window with attention sinks (SW+S) [30], H2O [29], and KIVI [48]. These methods fall below 50% aggregate RULER accuracy at 8K context and degrade rapidly at longer contexts, despite the 4096-token budget for sliding window and H2O. We note that SAGA could readily combine with KV cache quantization and eviction methods, such as those used for comparison above. Furthermore, to experiment at a larger scale, we evaluate Llama3.1-70B-Instruct [39] with AtopN only. Since AtopN requires no retraining, these experiments remain feasible with limited computational resources. To highlight that the AtopN approximation holds over long generations, we evaluate on tasks that elicit extended outputs before a final answer: GPQA Main, limited to 8K output tokens, and GPQA Diamond, limited to 16K output tokens [49]. Both consist of graduate-level questions spanning multiple domains, however Diamond is a harder subset. 6 Limitations, discussion and conclusion 

We introduced SAGA, an attention architecture designed around the computational trade-offs of sparse decoding. By decoupling key and value head counts, SAGA directly addresses the query-key bottleneck that dominates under sparse attention, while preserving model capacity through additional value heads. Our experiments show that SAGA nearly matches equivalent GQA models when trained from scratch and retains 96.3% of the teacher’s aggregate RULER score when distilled from pretrained checkpoints, while delivering substantial inference end-to-end speedups of more than 2×

at long contexts. Several limitations point to promising future directions. First, our current implementation relies on the simple Atop-N method; integrating hardware-aware sparse attention kernels could yield further speedup gains. Second, our distilled SAGA models maintain the same architecture as the teacher aside from the reduced key projections — future work could compensate for this slight capacity reduction by reallocating parameters, for example by increasing the value head count or dimension. Finally, our quality experiments are limited to models of up to 1.5B parameters; validating SAGA’s benefits in a full-scale pretraining setting remains an important open question. More broadly, we believe that co-designing attention architectures with their inference-time sparsity patterns is a fertile direction, and hope that SAGA serves as a step toward making this a standard consideration in LLM design.

C Implementation details 

Code implementation of our experiments is included in https://github.com/noamelata/SAGA .

C.1 Atop-N attention 

Our Atop-N attention is an efficient implementation of the algorithm described in Sec. 3.1. Specifi-cally, it uses a pre-calibrated per-head threshold to collect all entries that exceed it, greatly improving runtimes. This comparison operation uses a custom portable Triton 2 kernel, replacing pytorch’s CUDA-optimized torch.topk used for true top-N values. During the first decode step after prefill, we compute exact attention probabilities via softmax and extract the N -th largest value per head using torch.topk . This value serves as the threshold for the subsequent n decode steps. Because the attention distribution changes slowly across consecutive decode steps, we use n = 32 , thus amortizing the computational cost of torch.topk across 32 decode steps. Alongside with the reduced probability-value multiplication, computing softmax only on the top-N values is a significant source of speedup. Yet, we find that thresholds in logit space tend to perform poorly. Instead, we make use of a probability-space threshold τ transformed into the logit space, a much cheaper but mathematically equivalent operation. Given raw attention logits z, we approximate the max logit ˆm from a small tail of positions and compute the log-sum-exp denominator 

ˆs = P 

> i

exp( zi − ˆm). The logit-space threshold is then ˆm + log( τ · ˆs), which requires only a single reduction pass over the full sequence rather than a full softmax. The selected indices are used to gather the corresponding value vectors, after which the logit-space weights are normalized via softmax (the −∞ padding receives zero probability) and multiplied with the gathered values via regular batched matrix multiplication (for batch < 16) or sparse batched matrix multiplication (otherwise) to produce the attention output. Certain layers can be designated as exempt from Top-N selection and always use full attention. In our experiments, we exempt the first and 14th attention layers for Qwen2.5-1.5B 3 and the first layer for Llama 3.2-1B 4, as these layers tend to have more diffuse attention patterns. The threshold comparison and index collection are implemented as a single Triton kernel. Each row of the attention weight matrix is partitioned into blocks (autotuned over 512, 1024, and 2048 elements). Within each block, elements exceeding the threshold are identified via a comparison mask, their positions computed via an intra-block cumulative sum, and results written to an output buffer using an atomic counter to coordinate across blocks. The kernel outputs both the selected values and their indices into a fixed-size buffer of capacity 2N , with unused slots initialized to −∞ . If the number of entries exceeding the threshold exceeds 2N , the atomic counter saturates and excess entries are silently dropped, preserving memory safety. The buffer is allocated once and reused across steps, ensuring compatibility with CUDA graph capture.

17 C.2 Training SAGA from scratch 

We use the SmolLM2-360M 5 backbone. All models are trained on SlimPajama 6 for 20K steps with batch size 256 and sequence length 1024, totaling ∼5.24B tokens. We use the AdamW optimizer [50] with (β1, β 2) = (0 .9, 0.999) and weight decay 0.1 and a cosine learning rate schedule with 500 warmup steps, peak learning rate 3 × 10 −4, and minimum learning rate 7.5 × 10 −5. Training is performed in bfloat16, and each configuration is run with 3 seeds. 

C.3 Distillation to SAGA 

The student model is initialized from the teacher by merging the key projection heads according to the procedure described in Section 4.1. We then reorder the teacher’s query, value, and output projection heads using the induced permutations described in Section B so that they remain aligned with the merged key heads, and copy all parameters unchanged from the teacher. The student is trained with a weighted combination of KL divergence from the teacher’s output distribution (weight α = 0 .7,temperature T = 3 ) and hard-label cross-entropy (weight 1 − α = 0 .3), using AdamW with fused implementation. Training proceeds in three stages with increasing context length. In Stage 1, the student trains for 15K steps at context length 4096 with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −4 and global batch size 64. The learning rate schedule follows a warmup-stable-decay (WSD) pattern: 200 warmup steps, 9800 stable steps, and 5000 decay steps. The data mix is 75% SlimPajama (packed), 15% QA data (HotpotQA 7 and SQuAD 8, packed), and 10% synthetic needle-in-a-haystack examples. Stage 2 starts from the Stage 1 checkpoint with a fresh optimizer and trains for 5K steps at context length 20480 with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 and global batch size 32. The WSD schedule uses 200 warmup, 3300 stable, and 1500 decay steps. The data mix shifts to 75% SlimPajama, 5% QA, 10% long-context documents (LongAlign-10k 9, LongAlpaca-12k 10 , individually padded), and 10% synthetic NIAH. Stage 3 continues from the Stage 2 checkpoint and trains at context length 36864 with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5 and global batch size 32. For Qwen2.5-1.5B: 2K steps, α = 0 .7, T = 3 , WSD schedule with 100 warmup and 500 decay steps. For Llama 3.2-1B: 4K steps, α = 0 .85 , T = 4 ,WSD schedule with 200 warmup and 800 decay steps. Stage 3 interleaves 20K-length microbatches (2 per gradient step) with the 36K-length data to prevent forgetting of shorter-range attention patterns. Training at 36864 tokens provides headroom over the 32K evaluation target. Total training time is approximately 60 hours on 8 ×H200 GPUs.

Quality is evaluated using the RULER benchmark 11 for long-context tasks, and HellaSwag 12 and WikiText 13 for general capabilities. All evaluations use the lm-eval-harness framework 14 .
