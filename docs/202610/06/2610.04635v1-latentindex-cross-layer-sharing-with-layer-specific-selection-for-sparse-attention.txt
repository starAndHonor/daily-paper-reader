## ABSTRACT 

Sparse attention reduces core-attention computation, but its indexers still incur re-peated selection work and per-layer key-cache storage. Reusing selected indices across layers reduces this overhead but constrains multiple layers to the same to-ken set. We introduce LatentIndex, which extends the latent-sharing principle of Multi-head Latent Attention across indexer layers. Each layer group constructs a shared latent cache from its first layer’s hidden states, while layer-specific scor-ing enables independent token selection. Absorbing key decoders into queries enables direct scoring of the shared cache without reconstructing historical per-layer keys. We develop training-free calibration and investigate a training-aware instantiation of this principle. To balance quality and computation, a hierarchi-cal selection (HS) variant lets followers independently refine a shared candidate set proposed by the anchor. With four-layer sharing, LatentIndex reduces logi-cal indexer-cache storage by 61.1% on DeepSeek-V3.2. Across DeepSeek-V3.2 and GLM-5, training-free LatentIndex improves head-wise attention-mass recall over IndexCache by up to 3.28 percentage points while maintaining RULER and LongBench performance close to native DSA. HS further achieves 2.30–2.72× decode indexer speedups over DSA across 8K–128K contexts, retaining most of LatentIndex’s recall. LatentIndex offers a new perspective on cross-layer indexing: sharing continuous representations rather than discrete selections enables efficient reuse while preserving layer-specific token selection. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Long-context processing is increasingly important for large language models. Web-based agents and software-engineering workflows require models to integrate information across multiple interaction steps and diverse sources (Nakano et al., 2021; Jimenez et al., 2024), while extended reasoning further increases context lengths. These workloads make attention efficiency critical to inference speed and serving capacity. Dense attention requires O(L2) computation over a sequence of length 

L; during autoregressive decoding, each new query also attends to a growing history, increasing computation and KV-cache traffic. Sparse attention reduces this cost by restricting the tokens or blocks that each query attends to (Child et al., 2019; Beltagy et al., 2020; Kitaev et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025). DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) implements fine-grained token selection through a lightning indexer, which scores historical tokens and selects the top-k positions for core attention. This reduces core-attention complexity to O(Lk ) over the sequence. However, the indexer scans the full prefix at each layer, retaining quadratic prefill complexity and maintaining a separate key

Cross-layer redundancy offers an opportunity to reuse indexing information. IndexCache (Bai et al., 2026) reports substantial overlap between nearby layers’ top-k selections and exploits it by retain-ing indexers in a subset of Full layers while letting Shared layers reuse their selected indices. This reduces indexing computation but constrains multiple layers to the same token set, preventing follow-ers from adapting their selections to their own queries and relevance distributions. This motivates our central question: Can we exploit cross-layer redundancy while retaining layer-specific token selection? 

Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) (DeepSeek-AI, 2024) offers a useful design principle: a com-pact latent representation can be shared across attention heads while supporting head-specific atten-tion. We extend this principle to indexer layers with LatentIndex . Each group of consecutive layers constructs one shared latent cache from the hidden states of its first layer, termed the anchor. Layer-specific decoders define effective indexer keys for all group members, including the anchor, while each layer retains its own scoring and top-k selection. At inference time, the decoders are absorbed into queries, enabling direct scoring of the shared cache without reconstructing historical per-layer keys. This design replaces separate indexer key caches with a shared representation while leaving the main-attention KV caches unchanged. To further accelerate indexing while preserving layer-specific decisions, we introduce a hierarchical selection (HS) variant. The anchor scans the full prefix to form a broad candidate set, and each follower independently scores these candidates and selects its own top-k tokens. HS thus balances computational savings with layer-specific selection quality. We instantiate LatentIndex in both training-free and training-aware settings. Training-free calibra-tion uses offline ridge reduced-rank regression to jointly fit shared projections and layer-specific decoders, yielding a shared representation that serves the entire group. All fitted parameters remain fixed during inference. Training-aware adaptation begins with a frozen backbone, training each layer’s indexer to match its dense-attention distribution. It then updates the backbone through sparse language modeling while continuing attention-based supervision of the indexers, without changing the main-attention architecture. We evaluate LatentIndex in both settings. On DeepSeek-V3.2 and GLM-5, training-free LatentIn-dex improves head-wise attention-mass recall over IndexCache at every evaluated length from 4K to 128K, by up to 3.28 percentage points. Its aggregate RULER and LongBench scores exceed Index-Cache’s on both models and remain close to native DSA’s. With four-layer sharing on DeepSeek-V3.2, LatentIndex reduces logical indexer-cache storage by 61.1%. HS retains most of its recall while achieving 2.30 –2.72 × decode indexer speedups over DSA across 8K–128K, measured across all 61 indexers. Compared with IndexCache, HS improves average recall, RULER, and LongBench scores through additional layer-specific candidate scoring. Training experiments on DeepSeek-V2-Lite further show higher warmup recall and generally lower sparse-training language-modeling loss than IndexCache. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce LatentIndex, bringing MLA’s latent-sharing principle to sparse-attention in-dexers across layers. Shared latent caching reduces indexer-cache storage, while query-side decoder absorption enables direct scoring with layer-specific token selection. • We develop training-free calibration and investigate training-aware adaptation. We further introduce hierarchical selection to reduce repeated full-prefix scoring through shared can-didates and layer-specific refinement. • We demonstrate improved selection quality over index reuse, downstream performance close to native DSA, and a quality–latency trade-off through hierarchical selection. Train-ing experiments further show that shared latent indexers can be learned while maintaining language-model loss close to native DSA. 22 RELATED WORK 

Sparse Attention. Sparse attention reduces attention cost by restricting the positions accessed by each query through fixed patterns, local/global connectivity, or content-dependent selection (Child et al., 2019; Beltagy et al., 2020; Kitaev et al., 2020). Recent methods include Native Sparse Atten-tion (NSA), MoBA, and MiniMax Sparse Attention, which combine selective access with efficient attention execution (Yuan et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025; Lai et al., 2026). DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) uses a lightning indexer to select tokens for the main attention compu-tation. Although this reduces the main attention cost, the indexer still scores the full causal prefix for each query, retaining quadratic scoring complexity during prefill. 

Efficient Indexing for Sparse Attention. Existing approaches improve indexing efficiency through cross-layer reuse, restricted search, and reduced representation or head costs. Index-Cache (Bai et al., 2026) lets subsequent layers reuse an anchor layer’s top-k indices, while DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) includes a Reuse mode for the available main KV cache and its latest selection. SparDA (Fu et al., 2026) predicts the KV blocks needed by the next layer, enabling prefetching to overlap with current-layer execution. HISA (Xu et al., 2026b) selects candidate blocks before token-level refinement, and PIVOT (Liu et al., 2026) amortizes full-prefix scoring across nearby queries through a proxy query. Other designs select over compressed representations (Xu et al., 2026a), dynamically activate indexer heads (Zhou et al., 2026), or use fewer query heads (Qiu et al., 2026). LatentIndex explores cross-layer representation sharing as a basis for efficient indexing while preserving layer-specific token selection. 

Representation Sharing and Compression. Sharing continuous representations offers another route to reducing attention overhead. CLA (Brandon et al., 2024) and MLKV (Zuhri et al., 2025) share key/value heads across layers, while YOCO (Sun et al., 2024) constructs global KV represen-tations for reuse across its cross-decoder layers. KVSharer (Yang et al., 2024) explores post-hoc layer-wise cache sharing. Beyond direct reuse, xKV (Chang et al., 2025) jointly factorizes grouped-layer KV caches into a shared low-rank subspace, and CommonKV (Wang et al., 2025) factorizes adjacent-layer projection weights to facilitate latent-cache merging. Dimensional compression also supports efficient attention and retrieval: MLA (DeepSeek-AI, 2024) combines latent KV storage with query-side absorption of content-key projections, while Loki (Singhania et al., 2024) exploits low-dimensional key structure for token selection. LatentIndex extends representation sharing from main-attention KV representations to the key representations maintained across indexer layers.

Offline Calibration. We instantiate LatentIndex in a frozen pretrained model through offline cali-bration of the shared projections and layer-specific decoders. For each group, we collect the anchor hidden states and the native indexer keys of all group members at matching token positions. We treat the group members’ keys as joint targets and use ridge reduced-rank regression Izenman (1975) to jointly fit a shared projection from the anchor hidden states and a separate decoder for each layer. The objective minimizes key-reconstruction error under the prescribed latent-rank constraints, with ridge regularization on the resulting mappings. We fit the NoPE and RoPE branches separately. For NoPE, we concatenate the group members’ target keys and fit a mapping of rank at most rN with an output bias. For RoPE, we fit a bias-free complex reduced-rank mapping independently at each native frequency. Same-frequency decoding preserves compatibility with positional rotation. Both branches admit closed-form solutions, requir-ing no gradient-based optimization or updates to pretrained weights. The fitted projections generate the shared latent cache, and the fitted decoders define the query transformations described above. All fitted parameters remain fixed during inference. Appendix A provides more details.

We also study training LatentIndex on DeepSeek-V2-Lite. Training proceeds in two stages: dense-attention warmup learns the indexers with a frozen backbone, followed by sparse training that adapts the backbone and indexers using their selected routes. 

Training instantiation. In this instantiation, the anchor hidden state supplies both the shared key representation and the inputs to all layer-specific query and head-weight projections. Each layer uses its own NoPE decoder, query projection, and head-weight projection to compute relevance scores and independently select tokens. On DeepSeek-V2-Lite, we partition the 27 layers into nine consec-utive groups of three, with top-2048 selection. For this training instantiation, we apply LayerNorm to the projected NoPE latent in Eq. equation 1, use bias-free NoPE decoders, and fix the rotary de-coder to the identity. Because all routing inputs are available at the anchor, the group’s routes can be computed at its entrance and then consumed by their respective layers during sparse execution. 

Dense-attention warmup. We first freeze the pretrained backbone and train the indexers to match each layer’s dense attention. For each query, the teacher distribution averages the core-attention probabilities across heads, while the indexer distribution applies softmax to its scores over the full causal prefix. Training minimizes the teacher-to-indexer KL divergence over valid prediction posi-tions. Each layer’s dense-attention distribution supervises its LatentIndex scoring distribution. In IndexCache, all three layer-specific teacher distributions supervise the anchor indexer. The method-specific loss normalization is detailed in Appendix D.2. This stage learns token-selection scores from dense attention while leaving the backbone and its dense-attention computation unchanged. 

Sparse training. Starting from each method’s warmup checkpoint, we activate its top-2048 selec-tions and train the backbone with next-token language modeling, retaining the main-attention archi-tecture. The indexers continue to receive KL supervision, now using each layer’s sparse-attention probabilities, with both distributions normalized over the selected positions. Teacher targets and indexer inputs are detached from the backbone, and the top-k operation does not pass language-modeling gradients to the selector. Additional training details are provided in Appendix D.1. 3.4 STORAGE AND COMPUTATION 

Indexer cache storage. For a group of m layers, we store one dC -dimensional latent instead of 

m separate dI -dimensional keys, giving a payload ratio of dC /(md I ). Including FP8 scales and the independent native layer, our DeepSeek-V3.2 configuration reduces logical indexer-cache storage by 61.1%. The main-attention KV cache remains unchanged. 

Fixed parameters and computation. Projection and decoder parameters are independent of con-text length. We generate the shared latent once per group and absorb each layer’s decoder into its queries. Without hierarchical selection, each layer independently scores the full causal history, requiring O(HI Ld C ) work per query and O(HI L2dC ) over prefill. LatentIndex enables sparse-attention indexers to share continuous key representations across layers while retaining layer-specific token selection. Training-free results on DeepSeek-V3.2 and GLM-5 show higher attention-mass recall than index reuse and downstream performance close to native DSA, with a 61.1% reduction in logical indexer-cache storage on DeepSeek-V3.2. The recall advan-tage is particularly pronounced for followers at long contexts and persists with larger sharing groups. Hierarchical selection reduces repeated full-prefix scoring through shared candidates, achieving de-code indexer speedups while retaining layer-specific refinement. Training experiments provide com-plementary evidence that shared latent indexers can be learned while maintaining language-model loss close to DSA. Together, these results support cross-layer representation sharing as a way to reduce indexer storage while preserving selection quality, with hierarchical selection providing ad-ditional computational savings.

Sparse training loads each method’s own warmup checkpoint, activates its routes, and updates the backbone with next-token LM loss while continuing indexer supervision. We initialize new indexer linear weights from a zero-mean normal distribution with standard deviation 0.02, and LayerNorm weights and biases to one and zero, respectively. For sparse training, we use the same dataset, cached sample and shuffle indices, seed, and sequential sampler across methods. We use a global batch size of 256, microbatch size 1, data parallelism 64, and context parallelism 1, aligning sample slots at matched training steps. D.2 INDEXER CONSTRUCTION AND TRAINING OBJECTIVES 

Shared latent indexers. For each group, we project the anchor’s normalized hidden state into a 128-dimensional NoPE latent and a 64-dimensional RoPE key. We apply LayerNorm only to the NoPE latent and use layer-specific linear decoders to produce 64-dimensional NoPE keys. Each layer directly reuses the shared RoPE key, corresponding to an identity rotary decoder. We generate all three layers’ queries and head weights from the anchor hidden state using separate layer-specific projections. All linear projections are bias-free. After applying RoPE, we apply the same normalized Hadamard rotation to queries and keys. During sparse training, we compute three independent top-2048 selections at the group entrance for the corresponding layers to consume. During warmup, we evaluate each layer’s indexer output against its own dense-attention teacher using the cached anchor input.
