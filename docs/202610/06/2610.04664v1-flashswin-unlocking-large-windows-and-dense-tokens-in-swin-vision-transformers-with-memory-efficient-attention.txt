# Abstract 

High-resolution vision backbones have long been forced to trade away local token density to afford larger receptive fields. Hierarchical Swin transformers impose this compro-mise because standard windowed attention materializes an 

M 2 ×M 2 score matrix per window, incurring O(M 4) mem-ory as windows or token grids grow. Furthermore, Swin adds a learned relative-position bias elementwise to atten-tion scores, requiring full materialization of the score ma-trix and its gradient. This keeps Swin and SwinV2 trapped in a small-window( M = 8 , 16 ), coarse-token regime with patch size 4 × 4 (p = 4 ), limiting performance for fine-grained tasks. We introduce FLASH SWIN , which replaces standard windowed attention with a FlashAttention imple-mentation that computes exact softmax attention without materializing the score matrix, reducing per-window mem-ory from O(M 4) to O(M 2). This enables higher token density and larger receptive fields without inflating mem-ory overhead. Peak training memory becomes virtually flat in window size: at a 32 × 32 window, FLASH SWIN -T re-quires only 12 .4 GB—unchanged from 8 × 8—compared to 

70 /90 GB for SwinV2/V1-T. However, applying FlashAt-tention directly to Swin creates a trade-off: bypassing the score matrix precludes Swin’s additive relative-position bias, forfeiting spatial information in exchange for mem-ory efficiency. FLASH SWIN restores position information as window-local learnable 2D RoPE, making large win-dows and dense token grids both affordable and accurate. At matched scale, FLASH SWIN -T outperforms Swin vari-ants while improving out-of-domain generalization. With dense tokens and wide windows ( p = 2 , M = 32 ), the same Tiny model reaches 84 .1% ImageNet-1K, 44 .1 COCO box AP, and 47 .28 ADE20K mIoU—gains of +1 .3, +5 .1, and 

+1 .82 over SwinV2-T at M = 16 , respectively. At fixed 

M = 32 , halving the patch size yields roughly 3× larger gains in boundary quality than in mIoU. By removing mem-ory bottlenecks, FLASH SWIN unlocks a new scaling axis for swin transformers: combining dense tokens with large receptive fields. 

# 1. Introduction 

Fine-grained spatial detail is essential to a wide range of vision tasks, yet vision-transformer backbones [16] are de-signed for and trained at comparatively low resolution. Dense prediction tasks such as semantic segmentation and object detection depend on this detail to localize boundaries and resolve small structures [7, 26, 44, 45]. The gap is widest in domains whose native inputs dwarf the 224 2 or 

256 2 images used to pretrain modern backbones: gigapixel histopathology slides, volumetric medical scans, aerial im-agery, and document scans [11, 29, 33, 35, 42]. In each case the standard remedy is to discard resolution at the in-put itself. Patch embedding collapses a 16 × 16 region into a single token in ViT, a 4 × 4 region in Swin [26], and 

16 3 or 43 voxel blocks in 3D equivalents [2, 4, 42]. The size of these patches is dictated by the memory and com-pute cost of the backbone rather than by the granularity the task requires. Hierarchical windowed transformers such as Swin [26] were introduced precisely to relieve this pressure, restricting attention to local windows so that cost grows lin-early, rather than quadratically, in the number of tokens. That linear scaling, however, has not translated into high-resolution training. Three coupled choices govern how much spatial information a windowed transformer actually captures: the window size M , the patch size of the stem, and the input resolution. The latter two jointly fix the token grid—a finer patch or a larger input yields denser tokens that retain local structure and reduce the need for auxiliary refinement modules—while M fixes the region over which those tokens interact directly, before information must prop-agate through the hierarchy. Token density and window size act along complementary axes, and together they set 1arXiv:2610.04664v1 [cs.CV] 3 Oct 2026 the balance between local detail and contextual range avail-able to the model [15, 43]. In practice all three are kept small. Swin [26] and SwinV2 [27] train with narrow win-dows ( M = 7 -8 at pretraining resolution), a coarse patch-4

stem, and 224 2-256 2 inputs, fine-tuning to higher resolu-tion for only 30 epochs against 300 for pretraining, and at considerable cost. Crucially, these settings are not chosen for accuracy; they are forced by memory . Windowed atten-tion materializes an M 2 × M 2 score matrix per window, an 

O(M 4) footprint; summed over the HW/M 2 windows of a feature map this becomes O(HW M 2)—a factor of M 2

more entries than tokens, growing with the window and the token count, and therefore quadratically in the input side length. High-resolution training thus reaches a memory ceiling of the available hardware well before its accuracy gains are exhausted. The result is that dense token grids, wide windows, and native high-resolution training remain largely unexplored. FlashAttention [13] removes exactly this bottleneck. It computes the attention output incrementally using tiled on-line softmax without fully materializing the score matrix in memory, cutting the per-window footprint from O(M 4) to 

O(M 2). The kernel computes the same softmax attention, without approximation. Window size in particular stops driving activation memory, though token count still does. Swin, however, cannot adopt the FlashAttention kernel as a drop-in replacement. Its relative-position bias is added ele-mentwise to the M 2 ×M 2 score matrix, the exact tensor the fused kernel avoids forming to save memory. Retaining the bias forfeits memory savings, while dropping it degrades accuracy by removing intra-window positional awareness (Tab. S2). Nor does enlarging the bias help: Swin’s table covers only the (2 M − 1) 2 offsets seen in training, and although SwinV2’s continuous reformulation does gener-ate unseen offsets, it remains a learned term added to the 

M 2 × M 2 scores-and a learned term there needs its gradi-ent there. The obstacle is additivity, not coverage. Recov-ering Swin’s accuracy under exact, memory-efficient atten-tion therefore requires moving position out of the score ma-trix. We introduce F LASH SWIN , which leaves the hierarchy intact but replaces the two components that assume a mate-rialized matrix or a coarse grid: the position bias becomes a 2D RoPE applied to queries and keys before the FlashAt-tention kernel, continuous in the coordinate difference so it evaluates at any window size, with learned frequencies; the disjoint patch embedding becomes an overlapping one, preserving detail across patch boundaries at high token den-sity. The savings are large (Fig. 1C): at a 32 × 32 win-dow, F LASH SWIN -T trains in 12 .4GB against 70 .4GB for SwinV2-T, and it trains grids 4× denser than the standard patch-4 setting, which at this window exhaust memory for both Swin variants. At matched compute ( 5.9 GFLOPs), FLASH SWIN -T achieves 82 .3% ImageNet-1K top-1 accuracy, outperform-ing Swin baselines with gains transferring consistently to ADE20K and COCO. Scaling to dense tokens and wider windows ( p = 2 , M = 32 ) elevates the same 28 M back-bone to 84 .1% . However, clean classification understates the broader impact: F LASH SWIN -T improves robustness to adversarial, sketch, and corrupted inputs, while its relative gains on ADE20K boundary metrics are approximately 3×

larger than on mIoU. These results suggest that spatial res-olution has a particularly strong effect on robustness and fine localization. This denser regime increases compute to 

37 .4 GFLOPs, but remains trainable at batch sizes where standard windowed attention exceeds memory limits and re-quires activation checkpointing, which saves memory at the cost of computation [6]. Our contributions are: • F LASH SWIN : Exact fused attention for the hierarchi-cal networks. Window-local 2D RoPE and overlapping tokens make standard FlashAttention usable inside Swin. FLASH SWIN -T achieves 82 .3% ImageNet-1K top-1 at matched compute ( 5.9 GFLOPs, vs. 81 .9% SwinV2-T), with better out of distribution generalization. • Spatial resolution governs boundary fidelity and ro-bustness. Our results show that contextual range dictates region classification, while token density dic-tates boundary localization—yielding 3× larger rela-tive boundary gains on ADE20K than mIoU. At fixed 

28 M capacity, expanding spatial resolution boosts out-of-distribution accuracy, a pattern that scales con-sistently to large models (≈ 197 M parameters). https://github.com/tushaarkataria/FlashSwin. 

# 2. Related Works 

Hierarchical and windowed vision transformers. Vi-sion Transformers apply self-attention over image patches, but quadratic complexity makes high-resolution process-ing expensive [15, 16, 26, 37, 43]. Hierarchical architec-tures address this by progressively reducing spatial resolu-tion and restricting attention to local regions. Swin [26] uses shifted non-overlapping windows to achieve linear complexity, while SwinV2 [27] improves scaling through normalization, attention changes, and continuous relative-position biases. Others redesign the attention neighbor-hood: Focal Transformer [43] combines local interactions with coarse global context, CSWin [15] uses cross-shaped windows, and NAT [17] uses sliding neighborhoods. Acomplementary line reduces token counts through token merging [3], simplified hierarchies [32], or post hoc feature pyramids [24]. In contrast, F LASH SWIN retains Swin’s hi-erarchy and attention pattern, targeting memory costs within 

each window so window size and token density can expand rather than economize—preserving fine-grained spatial de-tails without prohibitive memory overhead. 

Memory-efficient attention and positional encodings. 

2FlashAttention [12, 13] preserves exact softmax attention through IO-aware tiling without materializing the attention matrix. Integrating fused attention into windowed vision transformers, however, is complicated by Swin’s additive score-level positional bias. One approach is to modify the fused kernel itself: FlashBias [39] incorporates additive bi-ases during tiled attention, although its default formulation is not end-to-end trainable and requires a low-rank approx-imation for training. An alternative is to move positional modeling outside the kernel by encoding position directly in queries and keys. Following this second direction, Heo et al. [22] extend RoPE to vision using 2D positional co-ordinates and learnable mixed frequencies. FLASH SWIN 

builds on this formulation but uses a single frequency set shared across both spatial axes and attention heads, with positions indexed locally within each window. This yields a window-size-independent positional representation that ap-plies identically to regular and shifted windows and remains compatible with standard FlashAttention primitives. Our contribution is therefore not a new positional encoding it-self, but using this formulation to make Swin fully train-able with fused attention and to study the resulting large-window, dense-token regime. 

Tokenization and spatial detail. Tokenization deter-mines how much fine-grained spatial structure enters the transformer backbone. Standard models like ViT and Swin embed non-overlapping patches ( p × p convolution with stride p), creating hard borders where high-frequency edge information and local continuity are permanently lost [16, 26]. Subsequent architectures adopt overlapping or convo-lutional tokenization to preserve local context across bound-aries, as in PVTv2 [38]. In our setting, this boundary frag-mentation is exacerbated in the dense-token regime ( p = 2 ), where the number of patch borders quadruples. FLASH -SWIN pairs window-local RoPE with an overlapping patch stem ( 2p − 1 kernel, stride p, padding p − 1), ensuring that both spatial position and high-frequency boundary detail survive the transition to fused attention and dense tokens. 

# 3. Method 

While preserving Swin’s macro-hierarchy, FLASH SWIN 

updates three core components: it replaces standard win-dowed attention with FlashAttention to bypass score-matrix materialization, restores lost spatial awareness via a window-local learnable 2D RoPE on queries and keys(Figure 1B), and adopts overlapping token embeddings to preserve fine spatial structure (Figure 1A).

The computation is tiled and the softmax is accumulated online, so the complete N × N matrix is never stored. This re-duces auxiliary attention memory from O(N 2) to O(N ) per window, equivalently from O(M 4) to O(M 2), and stage-level storage to approximately O(HW ), largely indepen-dent of window size. Only the implementation of attention changes: the same softmax attention is computed without approximation. While FlashAttention is widely used for long-sequence processing, its implications for scaling win-dow size and token density in hierarchical vision transform-ers remain comparatively underexplored. The bias term 

B in Eq. (1) is the obstacle to this substitution, since it is added elementwise to the score matrix the fused kernel never forms. Removing it outright costs accuracy (Tab. S2), so we instead encode position in the queries and keys.

Under the shifted-window configuration, co-ordinates are recomputed relative to each window’s own ori-gin after the cyclic shift, so displacements remain window-local and the shift introduces no discontinuity in the encod-ing. Attention masking for wrapped windows is applied as an additive mask passed to the fused kernel, which supports masking without materializing the full score matrix. Unlike Swin’s discrete bias table, this parameteriza-tion is independent of the window size M . Swin stores 

(2 M − 1) 2 bias entries per head— 3969 at M =32 —defined only over the offsets seen during training, whereas Eq. (2) uses d/ 2 frequencies that are continuous in the coordinate difference. The same learned frequencies can therefore be evaluated at any window size or input resolution without interpolation, which matters here because window size is varied explicitly as a scaling dimension. 

Overlapping Token Embedding. Swin tokenizes the input with a non-overlapping patch projection, implemented as a convolution with kernel size and stride p. This creates hard boundaries between neighboring patches, separating local structures that cross a patch border before they enter the transformer. FLASH SWIN replaces this stem with an overlapping token embedding: a strided convolution with kernel size 2p − 1, stride p, and padding p − 1. The stride is unchanged, so output resolution and token count are identi-cal to the standard patch-p embedding, but the larger kernel gives adjacent tokens overlapping receptive fields and al-lows them to share context across patch boundaries. The modification is most relevant in the dense-token regime, where a smaller patch preserves finer detail but also mul-tiplies the boundaries at which it can be lost. The stem adds a lightweight local inductive bias while leaving the rest of the hierarchy unchanged. 

Resulting Design Space. These three components are individually simple, yet jointly essential. Fused attention eliminates the O(M 4) window-scaling bottleneck, but can-not be used alongside Swin’s additive bias. Window-local RoPE provides positional awareness in a kernel-compatible, scale-invariant form. Finally, the overlapping stem miti-gates spatial boundary fragmentation caused by finer tok-enization. Removing any single component forfeits either memory savings or representation accuracy (Tab. S2).While fused attention improves throughput, its key architectural impact is decoupling activation memory from window size 

M . While total token count remains bounded by spatial resolution, window expansion is no longer constrained by memory spikes. This allows F LASH SWIN to scale local re-ceptive fields for substantially larger context modeling with-out memory overhead. The following experiments evaluate these expanded operating points ( M ∈ [8 , 32] , patch size 

P ∈ [2 , 4] ) across core vision tasks. 

# 4. Results and Discussion 

Experimental setup. We evaluate FLASH SWIN on ImageNet-1K classification [31], COCO detection [25], and ADE20K segmentation [46]. Unless stated other-wise, all experiments use the Tiny configuration matched to Swin-T and SwinV2-T ( C=96 , depths {2, 2, 6, 2}, heads 

{3, 6, 12 , 24 }, ≈28 M parameters); the four-stage hierarchy, patch merging, and alternating regular and shifted win-dows are unchanged from Swin. We vary window size 

M ∈ { 8, 16 , 32 } and patch size p ∈ { 4, 2}, training all models under the standard Swin recipe [26] with matched schedules and optimization settings. The SwinV1/V2-T 4baselines are re-trained (locally) at 256 2 under this iden-tical recipe. All models, including baselines, are trained under PyTorch AMP autocast ( fp16 ). F LASH SWIN ’s fused attention consumes bf16 queries, keys, and values, applies the rotary embedding in fp32 , and accumulates the softmax in fp32 inside the kernel, returning an fp32 output without ever materializing the N × N attention matrix. The Swin V1/V2 baselines compute A = QK ⊤ and AV in fp16 with the softmax promoted to fp32 by autocast, and must ma-terialize the attention matrix because their relative position bias is an additive term on it. Peak memory for the models is measured over a full forward and backward pass at batch size 128 and 256 ×256 input on an H200, without gradient checkpointing, unless noted otherwise. Classification mod-els are trained on four H200 GPUs, and downstream models for segmentation and detection are trained on four and two A100-40GB GPUs, respectively. We additionally compare against the trainable low-rank formulation of FlashBias [39] to evaluate an alternative approach that retains additive po-sitional bias within fused attention. Additional implemen-tation details are provided in Section S1.1. 

Robustness. We evaluate the trained IN-1K models without fine-tuning on ImageNet-A [21], -R [20], and -Sketch [36] (top-1 over the standard 200 -class subsets for A and R), and ImageNet-C [19] (AlexNet-normalized mean corruption error, mCE, over 15 corruptions at 5 severities). We evaluate the released 224 2 ImageNet-C images with the standard transform rather than regenerating corruptions at 

256 2; the resulting 1.30 × upsampling makes absolute mCE incomparable to the 224 protocol but is applied identically to every model. 

Downstream transfer. For semantic segmentation, we evaluate UPerNet [41] on ADE20K. Beyond standard mIoU, we report Boundary-IoU [8] and boundary F1 [10] near object contours, where larger windows and denser to-kens exert the greatest impact. For object detection, we evaluate Mask R-CNN [18] on COCO [25]. To keep the dense p=2 setting tractable, inputs are uniformly padded to 

512 ×512 rather than 1333 ×800 . Window size M is fixed to its pretraining value throughout, meaning larger inputs increase window count rather than window scale. Full im-plementation and training details are provided in the Sup-plementary S1.1. 

## 4.1. Memory Scaling 

Figure 1C, Figure S1, and Table S1 show the central ef-ficiency result: F LASH SWIN activation memory is largely decoupled from window size, and so is throughput. At patch size 4, F LASH SWIN -T remains at 12 .4 GB as M grows from 8 to 32 , while Swin-T increases from 15 .7 to 90 .1 GB (5.7×) and SwinV2-T from 15 .0 to 70 .4 GB. The baselines pay for larger windows in both memory and speed: Swin-T falls from 1173 to 442 img/s and SwinV2-T from 871 to 

371 , whereas F LASH SWIN -T remains near 1K img/s. At 

M =8 , Swin-T is slightly faster because the small attention matrix does not amortize fused-kernel overhead; F LASH -SWIN overtakes both baselines from M =16 and is 2.4×

faster than Swin-T at M =32 . FlashBias is likewise flat in 

M , confirming that fusion, rather than the positional encod-ing, removes the window dependence; however, F LASH -SWIN uses less memory, becomes faster at larger windows, and remains fixed at 28 .3M parameters while FlashBias grows with M .Denser grids move the baselines closer to the hardware ceiling. At patch size 2, F LASH SWIN -T holds at 47 .7 GB and ∼290 img/s across windows, while both Swin vari-ants exceed 120 GB at M =16 . At M =32 , Swin-T can-not train even with checkpointing [6], and SwinV2-T runs only with checkpointing at 138 .9 GB and 57 img/s, against FLASH SWIN ’s 47 .7 GB and 272 img/s without it— 2.9×

less memory at 4.8× the throughput. FlashBias[39] remains memory-stable at 51 .5 GB but reaches only 205 img/s, while its M =8 configuration cannot launch at batch 128 

because of its kernel grid limit. Checkpointing further re-duces memory but trades it for recomputation, making the ability to train directly consequential for throughput. Fi-nally, the 4× increase in token count from patch 4 to 2 costs FLASH SWIN only 3.85 × the memory ( 12 .4 → 47 .7 GB), while increasing M adds essentially none. Thus, F LASH -SWIN shifts the memory scaling from window size to to-ken count: standard windowed attention becomes memory-bound long before large windows cease to be useful, and that is the constraint F LASH SWIN removes.

To test whether the benefits of F LASH SWIN persist with increasing model capacity, we scale F LASH SWIN -L to approximately 197 M parameters, closely matching Swin-L [26] and SwinV2-L [27]. remains beneficial as model capacity increases.

## 4.6. Token Density via finer patches vs. higher res. 

Because F LASH SWIN decouples memory from window size, a target token density can be reached two ways: a finer patch embedding at low input resolution, or a coarser patch at high resolution. Table S6 contrasts the two at a fixed 

128 × 128 token grid with matched FLOPs and parameters. The two routes are nearly interchangeable on these bench-marks. They are near-identical in what they learn ( 84 .1 vs. 

84 .0 top-1, with the finer patch +0 .5 ahead on ImageNet-V2) and, at equal batch size, near-identical in what they cost: 24 .4 vs. 23 .8GB of training memory and 287 vs. 284 

images/s. This is expected-beyond the first reduction the two networks are architecturally identical, and the stem is too small a fraction of activations to separate them-but it is worth establishing, because it isolates token count as the variable that matters. We state the conclusion narrowly. ImageNet-1K has a median short side of 374 pixels and only 6.4% of images reach 512 , so the coarse-patch arm is trained largely on upsampled data. The experiment therefore shows that, at these resolutions, a coarse stem on an interpolated image can match a finer stem on the original; it does not establish that native high-resolution input is unnecessary. The more consequential setting is data whose task-relevant signal ex-ists at the native pixel scale, such as cellular morphology in histopathology or small lesions and thin structures in med-ical imaging [1, 14, 23, 40]. Evaluating F LASH SWIN in these genuinely high-resolution regimes is an important di-rection for future work. 

# 5. Conclusion and Future Work 

In this work, we present F LASH SWIN , an architecture that removes the window-size memory wall in hierar-chical vision transformers. By replacing score-matrix relative-position bias with window-local learnable 2D RoPE, F LASH SWIN enables standard FlashAttention within windowed self-attention and makes previously impractical large-window, dense-token regimes ( M =32 , p=2 ) train-able. Our comparison with FlashBias[39] further shows that fused attention itself is sufficient to flatten memory scal-ing with window size, but not to reproduce the downstream gains of F LASH SWIN . Across classification, detection, seg-mentation, and distribution shift, larger windows improve contextual modeling while denser tokens provide particu-larly strong gains in fine spatial localization. These bene-fits persist from Tiny ( 28 M) to Large ( 197 M) models, with FLASH SWIN -L reaching 52 .58 mIoU on ADE20K and out-performing both Swin-L and SwinV2-L. Together, our re-sults show that scaling vision transformers is constrained not only by parameter count, but also by the spatial resolu-tion that hardware memory allows them to process. 

Limitations and future work. Although F LASH SWIN re-moves the dominant memory dependence on window size, dense tokenization still increases computation; the p=2 

configuration therefore trades additional FLOPs for spa-tial resolution. Our current benchmarks also operate at relatively modest image resolutions and do not fully test the regime that motivates native high-resolution process-ing. Extending F LASH SWIN to gigapixel histopathology, high-resolution medical imaging [1, 14, 23, 40], and other domains where downsampling removes task-relevant detail is therefore an important direction. Dense-token, large-window backbones may also benefit vision-language mod-els requiring fine-grained visual grounding and text recog-nition. Finally, extending memory-efficient windowed at-tention to 3D medical volumes and video is particularly promising, where the growth of volumetric attention makes the memory constraint even more severe. 8Acknowledgments

## S1.1. Additional Implementation Details 

Effective window per stage for FLASH SWIN . The win-dow size M reported throughout is the stage-1 value. When a stage’s spatial resolution is not larger than M , the im-plementation clamps the window to that resolution and dis-ables the shift, since a window larger than the feature map has no meaning. At 256 2 input with p=4 the stage grids are 

64 , 32 , 16 , 8, so M =32 is realized as 32 , 32 , 16 , 8 across the four stages, and M =64 at 512 2 is realized as 128 → 64 

only from stage 2 onward. The deepest stages therefore per-form global attention within their own resolution. This af-fects all three architectures identically and matches Swin’s own convention, but it must be reproduced for the reported numbers to match: an implementation that instead pads to a multiple of M will give different results at large M .

ADE20K Segmentation. Experiments are implemented in mmsegmentation [9] using UPerNet [41] initialized from ImageNet-1K checkpoints. We train using the standard 

160 K schedule with 512 ×512 crop sizes. Optimization uses AdamW with a learning rate of 1.2×10 −4 and weight de-cay of 0.01 . The effective batch size is 32 (4 GPUs × 4

images/GPU with 2 gradient-accumulation steps). 

COCO Detection. Experiments are implemented in 

mmdetection [5] using Mask R-CNN [18] under the stan-dard 1× schedule. Optimization uses AdamW with a learn-ing rate of 10 −4, weight decay of 0.05 , a 1000 -iteration warmup, and step decay at epochs 8 and 11 . The effective batch size is 16 (2 GPUs × 4 images/GPU with 2 gradient-accumulation steps). Inputs are padded to 512 ×512 across all models for uniform evaluation; absolute AP scores re-flect this reduced input resolution. 

## S1.2. Boundary tolerance sensitivity 

The BF-score depends on a matching tolerance τ , expressed as a fraction of the image diagonal d. Any single choice of τ

is arbitrary, and an improvement visible at one tolerance is not by itself evidence of better boundary quality. We there-fore sweep τ /d ∈ { 0.002 , 0.004 , 0.008 , 0.016 , 0.032 } over the full 2,000-image ADE20K validation set, holding the models, the predictions, and the Boundary-IoU band fixed. Table S4 reports the result. The ordering of the seven backbones is identical at all five tolerances, so the boundary result is a property of the models rather than of the operating point. Beyond this in-variance, the margin has a systematic shape: F LASH SWIN -T at 32 /2 leads the next-best model by +1 .50 BF at 

τ /d =0 .004 and by only +0 .84 at the loosest tolerance, de-caying as τ grows past the strictest settings. A larger τ

relaxes the criterion toward ordinary region overlap, so a margin that shrinks with τ is what one expects when the im-provement comes from more precise boundary localization rather than from better region classification. Consistently, the patch-size reduction that yields only +0 .40 mIoU yields 

+0 .87 Boundary-IoU and +1 .34 BF at τ /d = 0 .008 — two to three times as much gain at the boundary as in aggregate region accuracy, detail that mIoU alone substantially under-reports. 

Implementation. Both metrics accumulate per-class nu-merators and denominators across the dataset and reduce once at the end, matching the way mIoU is reported and re-maining stable for rare classes. Boundary-IoU uses a band of width 0.02 d around each class boundary and is indepen-dent of τ by construction, which the constant B-IoU column confirms. Predictions are single-scale, taken from the same checkpoints as Table 3. All models are scored on the iden-tical set of 150 classes observed by every model; restricting to this common set is necessary because a class predicted by one model but absent from the ground truth contributes zero to that model’s mean, which would otherwise penalize models that predict a wider range of classes. 

Per-class boundary analysis . Aggregate boundary met-rics do not indicate where the improvement arises, so we decompose the density effect per class. Per-class estimates are strongly frequency-dependent in variance: among the

## S1.5. What the ROPE learnable frequencies learn. 

Because the rotary frequencies are optimized rather than fixed, we can inspect what they converge to. Figure S4 plots the learned per-stage inverse-frequency spectrum against the geometric initialization. The frequencies do not remain at their initialized values: 84% move by more than 10% ,and—rather than a single global adjustment—each stage settles on a distinct profile, with the largest departures in the mid-frequency band. This indicates that the positional spec-trum best suited to windowed attention is stage-dependent, which a fixed schedule cannot capture, and provides direct 13 Figure S2.
